# Phrases & Formulas

| Old English Phrase | Modern Gloss | Text / Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hwæt! We Gardena in geardagum | "Listen! We [have heard] of the Spear-Danes in days of yore" | Beowulf, l.1 | Famous opening formula; "Hwæt" functions as an attention-getting interjection, not a literal question |
| Wyrd oft nereð unfægne eorl, þonne his ellen deah | "Fate often spares an undoomed man, when his courage holds" | Beowulf, ll.572-573 | Classic gnomic statement on fate (see "wyrd" in vocabulary.md) |
| Hige sceal þē heardra, heorte þē cēnre, mōd sceal þē māre, þē ūre mægen lȳtlað | "Will shall be the harder, heart the keener, courage the greater, as our strength diminishes" | The Battle of Maldon | Byrhtwold's speech, spoken as the English shield-wall is collapsing near the poem's end — the most famous couplet of the poem, often called the finest statement of the Germanic heroic code |
| Ic geþancie þē, ðēoda Waldend, ealra þǣra wynna þe ic on worulde gebād | "I thank you, Ruler of nations, for all the joys that I experienced in the world" | The Battle of Maldon | Byrhtnoð's dying prayer after being fatally wounded in battle |
| Wyrd bið ful ārǣd | "Fate is wholly inexorable" | The Wanderer, l.5 | One of the most quoted lines in Old English poetry; compare "wyrd biþ swīþre" in The Seafarer below |
| Hwǣr cwōm mearg? Hwǣr cwōm mago? Hwǣr cwōm māþþumgyfa? | "Where has the horse gone? Where the young warrior? Where the treasure-giver?" | The Wanderer, ll.92-93 | The poem's famous "ubi sunt" ("where are they now") passage lamenting lost lords and warriors; echoed by later poets including Tolkien |
| Wyrd biþ swīþre, meotud meahtigra þonne ænges monnes gehygd | "Fate is stronger, the Measurer mightier, than any man's thought" | The Seafarer, ll.115-116 | Companion line to The Wanderer's "wyrd bið ful ārǣd" — both poems close on the same gnomic idea |
| Ongyrede hine þā geong hæleð — þæt wæs God ælmihtig — strang ond stīðmōd | "The young hero stripped himself then — that was God almighty — strong and resolute" | The Dream of the Rood | Christ is depicted as a warrior-hero willingly mounting the cross like a hero mounting a battle; one of the most celebrated images in Old English poetry |
| Rōd wæs ic ārǣred; āhōf ic rīcne Cyning, heofona Hlāford | "I was raised up as a cross; I lifted up the mighty King, the Lord of heaven" | The Dream of the Rood | The cross itself speaking, describing the crucifixion in its own voice (prosopopoeia) |
| Nū sculon herigean heofonrīces Weard, meotodes meahte and his mōdgeþanc | "Now we must praise the Guardian of the heavenly kingdom, the might of the Measurer and his mind-plan" | Cædmon's Hymn, ll.1-2 | Opening of the oldest datable Old English poem (composed c.658-680), recorded by Bede in his Ecclesiastical History |
| Fore þǣm nēdfaerae nǣnig weorðeð þoncsnotturra | "Before that inevitable journey, no one becomes wiser in thought" | Bede's Death Song, l.1 | Traditionally said to have been composed by Bede himself on his deathbed in 735 AD — among the oldest attributed Old English poems |
| Hēr Æðelstān cyning, eorla drihten... geslōgon æt sæcce sweorda ecgum | "In this year King Æthelstan, lord of warriors... won in battle with the edges of swords" | The Battle of Brunanburh, ll.1-4 | The "Hēr" ("In this year") opening is the standard formula of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, into which this poem is inserted for the year 937 |
| Ne ðis ne dagað ēastan, ne hēr draca ne flēogeð | "This is not dawn in the east, nor does a dragon fly here" | The Finnsburh Fragment, l.3 | Hnæf's famous "false dawn" speech — he realizes the light and noise outside the hall are attackers' torches and shields, not sunrise |
| Hēr, hēr, hēr | "Here, here, here!" | Ælfric's Life of St Edmund | The martyred king's severed head calls out to guide searchers to it in the woods — one of the most famous miracle details in Old English hagiography |
| Rǣd sceal mid snyttro, ryht mid wīsum, til sceal mid tilum | "Counsel must go with wisdom, right conduct with the wise, good with good" | Maxims I, l.22 | Typical of the poem's "X sceal Y" gnomic formula stating what is fitting or proper (see "The sceal construction" in grammar.md) |
| Wynna gewītaþ, wera geswīcaþ | "Joys pass away, promises fail men" | The Rune Poem, l.94 (closing line, stanza for *Ear* "grave") | The poem's bleak closing note, after 29 stanzas moving from Feoh ("wealth") to Ear ("the grave") |
| Is þēos burch brēome geond Brēotenrīce | "This city is renowned throughout Britain" | Durham, l.1 | Opening line of one of the very last poems composed in the classical Old English alliterative style, c. 1104-1109 |
| þæt wæs gōd cyning | "That was a good king!" | Beowulf, l.11 | Famous formulaic understatement closing the description of Scyld Scefing — a hallmark of the poem's laconic narratorial voice |
| Moððe word frǣt | "A moth ate words" | Exeter Book Riddle 47 (Bookworm) | The famously terse opening line of one of the shortest and most-quoted riddles — a bookworm devouring a manuscript |
| Beorht wæron burgræced, burnsele monige | "Bright were the city-buildings, many the bath-houses" | The Ruin, l.21 | Marks the poem's turn from present ruin to a vivid memory of the city's former splendor, just before "mighty fate changed all that" a few lines later |
| Wa bið þam þe sceal, of langoþe leofes abidan | "Woe is it for the one who must, out of longing, wait for a beloved" | The Wife's Lament, ll.52-53 | The poem's closing couplet, generalizing the speaker's own grief into a universal maxim |
| þæt þu þær tirfæste treowe findest | "you will find glory-fast faithfulness there" | The Husband's Message, l.11 | The wooden messenger's core promise; *treowe* ("faith, troth") here is a different word from *treow* ("tree") — a fitting coincidence given a piece of wood is the one asking to be trusted (see *trēow* in vocabulary.md) |
| Hwylc is hæleþa þæs horsc ond þæs hygecræftig | "Who is a person so shrewd and so skilled in mind" | Exeter Book Riddle 1 (Storm) | The traditional riddle-challenge opening formula, daring the listener to name the speaker |
| Ic eom wunderlicu wiht, wifum on hyhte | "I am a wondrous creature, a joy to women" | Exeter Book Riddle 25 (Onion) | One of the Exeter Book's most-discussed riddles for its deliberate double meaning — the innocent solution (onion) and the bawdy one it's clearly also gesturing at both fit every line |
| Ut, spere, næs in, spere! | "Out, spear, not in, spear!" | Wið Færstice (metrical charm) | A variant of the charm's repeated expulsion-formula, addressed to whatever invisible "elf-shot" is causing the patient's sudden pain |
| Her com in gangan, in spiderwiht | "Here came walking in, a spider-creature" | Wið Dweorh (metrical charm) | The charm's vivid, unsettling opening image — scholars still debate whether "spider-creature" is literal or a poetic name for the fever itself |
| Matheus helm, Marcus byrne | "Matthew my helmet, Mark my mail-coat" | A Journey Charm | Part of the charm's striking image of the four Gospel-writers as pieces of armor — Luke becomes a sword and John a shield in the lines that follow |
| Sitte ge, sigewif, sigaþ to eorðan! | "Settle down, victory-women, sink to the earth!" | For a Swarm of Bees (metrical charm) | Addresses the swarming bees as *sigewif* ("victory-women"), a term otherwise used for the Valkyrie-like women of heroic poetry |
| genimeð him wulfas tō geferan | "takes wolves as companions for himself" | Maxims I | Describes the fate of a friendless, luckless man — compare *wineleas wrecca* ("friendless exile") in The Wife's Lament |
| Sohton sarigu tu sigebearn godes | "Sorrowful, the two sought God's victorious child" | The Descent into Hell, l.11 | The two Marys approaching Christ's tomb at dawn, in this poem's Old English retelling of the Harrowing of Hell |
