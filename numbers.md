# Numbers (Numerals)

Old English numerals behave less like a single uniform word class and
more like three different ones, depending on size. **1–3** decline like
adjectives, changing form for case, gender, and number to agree with the
noun they count (see the declension tables below) — this is a real
grammatical fossil of a much older Indo-European pattern. **4–19** are
normally indeclinable: they keep one form regardless of the case or
gender of the noun they count. **20 and up** (twentig, þrītig, hund,
þūsend, etc.) originally functioned as *nouns* in their own right,
governing the thing being counted in the genitive plural — "hund scipa"
literally means "a hundred of ships," the same construction as "a flock
of sheep" — though by the later Old English period they were already
drifting toward behaving like ordinary modern numbers. Ordinal numbers
(1st, 2nd, 3rd...) decline as weak adjectives, the same pattern used for
adjectives following a demonstrative (see "Adjective Declension" in
grammar.md).

Source: general Old English numeral grammar as commonly described in
reference grammars (Mitchell & Robinson, *A Guide to Old English*);
cross-checked against a student presentation, "Old English Numeral" by
Kateryna Lemeshuk (2020), for the specific word lists and declension
tables below. Spelling variants (e.g. *six, syx, siex*) reflect genuine
manuscript/dialect variation, not inconsistency in this table.
