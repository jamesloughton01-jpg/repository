# Futhorc Runes

Before adopting the Latin alphabet, the Anglo-Saxons — like other
Germanic-speaking peoples — wrote using runes: angular letters designed
for carving into wood, bone, horn, and stone rather than writing with
ink and pen. The English runic alphabet is called the **futhorc**, after
the sound-values of its first six characters (F, U, Þ, O, R, C), and it
developed out of the 24-character Elder Futhark used across
Germanic-speaking Europe. After migrating to Britain in the 5th century,
Anglo-Saxon scribes adapted that older alphabet to fit sound changes
already under way in their own dialects — splitting, for instance, one
Elder Futhark vowel-rune into three distinct English ones (ōs, āc, and
æsc) as those vowel sounds diverged — and adding new runes for sounds the
continental alphabet had no letter for. By the time of the *Old English
Rune Poem* (see *The Rune Poem* in the Texts tab, which devotes one
stanza to each character), the futhorc had grown to a standard 29 runes;
a handful of rare Northumbrian inscriptions extend the set further
still, to 31 or even 33.

Runes were never replaced overnight — for centuries, Anglo-Saxon England
used both writing systems side by side for different purposes. Christian
missionaries arriving from Rome at the end of the 6th century brought the
Latin alphabet along with Christianity itself, and it quickly became the
standard script for manuscripts, books, and formal documents, supplemented
with a few extra letters (þ, ð, æ, ƿ) borrowed or adapted from the runic
tradition to represent English sounds Latin had no letters of its own for
— the origin of the Special Letters table in the Alphabet tab. Runes
persisted alongside Latin script for shorter, more personal, or more
decorative purposes: carved memorial crosses (part of the text of *The
Dream of the Rood*, elsewhere in the Texts tab, is independently
preserved as a runic inscription on the Ruthwell Cross), inscribed
jewelry, coin legends, and the odd cryptic signature — Cynewulf famously
signed several of his poems by weaving his name into the verse in runes,
and *The Husband's Message* closes with five runes used as a kind of
oath-seal rather than spelled-out words. By the 11th century, everyday
runic literacy had become rare in England, though enough antiquarian
knowledge survived — through works like the Rune Poem itself — that
scribes could still describe and explain the runes centuries after they
had gone out of ordinary use.
