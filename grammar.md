# Grammar Rules

| Topic | Rule | Example | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Noun cases | Old English has four main cases — nominative (subject), accusative (direct object), genitive (possession), dative (indirect object/instrument) — plus a vestigial instrumental in some pronouns/adjectives | cyning (nom.) / cyning (acc.) / cyninges (gen.) / cyninge (dat.) | Mitchell & Robinson | Case is marked on nouns, adjectives, and determiners, not just word order |
| Noun gender | Every noun has grammatical gender (masc./fem./neut.), independent of natural sex | wif (neut.) "woman" | Mitchell & Robinson | Gender determines which declension endings apply |
| Verb classes | Verbs split into strong (form past tense via vowel gradation/ablaut) and weak (form past tense with a dental suffix, -de/-ede/-te) | strong: singan → sang; weak: fremman → fremede | Mitchell & Robinson | Strong verbs are further divided into seven ablaut classes |
| Adjective declension | Adjectives take "weak" endings after a demonstrative/possessive (definite context) and "strong" endings otherwise (indefinite context) | se goda cyning (weak) vs. god cyning (strong) | Mitchell & Robinson | Mirrors a pattern also found in other older Germanic languages |
| Word order | Main clauses tend toward verb-second order; subordinate clauses tend to be verb-final | — | Mitchell & Robinson | Word order is more flexible than Modern English since case marking carries grammatical roles |
| Personal pronouns | Old English retains a dual number ("we two" / "you two") alongside singular and plural, lost entirely in Modern English | wit "we two" / git "you two" (cf. "wit unc" — Dream of the Rood, l.132: "we two") | Mitchell & Robinson | Dual forms only exist for 1st/2nd person pronouns, not verbs or nouns |
| Negation | Ne precedes the verb, and contracts with certain common verbs: ne + wesan → nis, ne + habban → næfð, ne + witan → nāt | "Ne wāt ic mec beworhtne..." — Exeter Riddle 35, l.3 ("I do not know myself...") | Mitchell & Robinson | Old English also allows "multiple negation" (piling up ne/nān/nǣfre) for emphasis, unlike Modern English |
| Prepositions & case | Many prepositions govern a specific case, which determines the noun/pronoun ending that follows them | mid ("with") + dative; ofer ("over") + accusative; a few govern more than one case with a shift in meaning | Mitchell & Robinson | Worth memorizing preposition-case pairs early, since the case ending is often the only clue to a preposition's exact sense |
| The "sceal" construction | Sceal (lit. "shall/must") is used constantly in gnomic/wisdom poetry not to express obligation but what is fitting, natural, or characteristic of something | "Cyning sceal rīce healdan" — "a king shall [properly] hold a kingdom," not "a king is obliged to" | Mitchell & Robinson | The grammatical backbone of Maxims I and much of The Rune Poem; recognizing this idiomatic sense is essential to reading them correctly |
| Relative clauses | Formed with the indeclinable particle þe, or with a form of the demonstrative sē/sēo/þæt used relatively, sometimes combined as sē þe | "sē þe" = "he who," "þā þe" = "those who" | Mitchell & Robinson | Unlike Modern English "who/which/that," the relative marker never inflects for the case of its own clause |
| Kennings | Compound noun-phrases used as poetic circumlocutions in place of an ordinary word, a hallmark of Old English verse style | hronrād "whale-road" = the sea; bānhūs "bone-house" = the body; goldwine "gold-friend" = a lord | Mitchell & Robinson | Riddles are built almost entirely on extended kennings; recognizing the pattern is often the key to solving them |
