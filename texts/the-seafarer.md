# The Seafarer

Preserved in the Exeter Book. Old English text: sacred-texts.com, Cook & Tinker,
*Select Translations from Old English Poetry* (1902), public domain. Modern
English translation: own work for this repository, prose rendering (not verse).

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mæg ic be me sylfum soðgied wrecan, | I can speak a true tale about myself, |
| 2 | siþas secgan, hu ic geswincdagum | tell of journeys, how in toilsome days |
| 3 | earfoðhwile oft þrowade, | I have often suffered hardship, |
| 4 | bitre breostceare gebiden hæbbe, | endured bitter care in my breast, |
| 5 | gecunnad in ceole cearselda fela, | experienced in a ship many anxious dwellings, |
| 6 | atol yþa gewealc, þær mec oft bigeat | the terrible tossing of waves, where the narrow night-watch |
| 7 | nearo nihtwaco æt nacan stefnan, | often seized me at the ship's prow, |
| 8 | þonne he be clifum cnossað. Calde geþrungen | when it tosses near the cliffs. Pressed by cold |
| 9 | wæron mine fet, forste gebunden, | were my feet, bound by frost, |
| 10 | caldum clommum, þær þa ceare seofedun | in cold fetters, where cares sighed |
| 11 | hat ymb heortan; hungor innan slat | hot around my heart; hunger tore from within |
| 12 | merewerges mod. þæt se mon ne wat | the mind of one weary of the sea. The man does not know this |
| 13 | þe him on foldan fægrost limpeð, | for whom things happen most pleasantly on land — |
| 14 | hu ic earmcearig iscealdne sæ | how I, wretched with care, on the ice-cold sea |
| 15 | winter wunade wræccan lastum, | dwelt through winter on the paths of exile, |
| 16 | winemægum bidroren, | deprived of beloved kinsmen, |
| 17 | bihongen hrimgicelum; hægl scurum fleag. | hung about with icicles; hail flew in showers. |
| 18 | þær ic ne gehyrde butan hlimman sæ, | There I heard nothing but the roaring sea, |
| 19 | iscaldne wæg. Hwilum ylfete song | the ice-cold wave. At times the swan's song |
| 20 | dyde ic me to gomene, ganetes hleoþor | I took for my entertainment, the gannet's cry |
| 21 | ond huilpan sweg fore hleahtor wera, | and the curlew's call instead of men's laughter, |
| 22 | mæw singende fore medodrince. | the singing gull instead of the drinking of mead. |
| 23 | Stormas þær stanclifu beotan, þær him stearn oncwæð | Storms beat the stone-cliffs there, where the tern answered them, |
| 24 | isigfeþera; ful oft þæt earn bigeal, | icy-feathered; very often the eagle screamed around, |
| 25 | urigfeþra; ne ænig hleomæga | dewy-feathered; no protecting kinsman |
| 26 | feasceaftig ferð frefran meahte. | could comfort my desolate spirit. |
| 27 | Forþon him gelyfeð lyt, se þe ah lifes wyn | Therefore he little believes it, he who has the joy of life, |
| 28 | gebiden in burgum, bealosiþa hwon, | experienced among cities, and few terrible journeys, |
| 29 | wlonc ond wingal, hu ic werig oft | proud and flushed with wine, how I, weary, often |
| 30 | in brimlade bidan sceolde. | had to endure on the sea-path. |
| 31 | Nap nihtscua, norþan sniwde, | The shadow of night grew dark, it snowed from the north, |
| 32 | hrim hrusan bond, hægl feol on eorþan, | frost bound the ground, hail fell on the earth, |
| 33 | corna caldast. Forþon cnyssað nu | coldest of grains. Therefore now they trouble, |
| 34 | heortan geþohtas, þæt ic hean streamas, | the thoughts of my heart, that I myself should test |
| 35 | sealtyþa gelac sylf cunnige; | the tossing of the salt waves; |
| 36 | monað modes lust mæla gehwylce | the desire of my spirit constantly urges |
| 37 | ferð to feran, þæt ic feor heonan | my spirit to journey, so that I, far from here, |
| 38 | elþeodigra eard gesece. | should seek the land of foreign people. |
| 39 | Forþon nis þæs modwlonc mon ofer eorþan, | Therefore there is no man on earth so proud of spirit, |
| 40 | ne his gifena þæs god, ne in geoguþe to þæs hwæt, | nor so generous with his gifts, nor so bold in his youth, |
| 41 | ne in his dædum to þæs deor, ne him his dryhten to þæs hold, | nor so brave in his deeds, nor his lord so gracious to him, |
| 42 | þæt he a his sæfore sorge næbbe, | that he never has anxiety about his sea-voyage, |
| 43 | to hwon hine dryhten gedon wille. | as to what the Lord will do to him. |
| 44 | Ne biþ him to hearpan hyge ne to hringþege, | He has no thought for the harp, nor for receiving rings, |
| 45 | ne to wife wyn ne to worulde hyht, | nor joy in a woman, nor delight in the world, |
| 46 | ne ymbe owiht elles, nefne ymb yða gewealc, | nor in anything else but the tossing of the waves, |
| 47 | ac a hafað longunge se þe on lagu fundað. | but he who is bound for the sea always has longing. |
| 48 | Bearwas blostmum nimað, byrig fægriað, | The groves take on blossoms, the towns grow fair, |
| 49 | wongas wlitigað, woruld onetteð; | the fields grow beautiful, the world hastens on; |
| 50 | ealle þa gemoniað modes fusne | all these things urge the eager-hearted man's |
| 51 | sefan to siþe, þam þe swa þenceð | spirit to the journey, in him who so intends |
| 52 | on flodwegas feor gewitan. | to travel far on the paths of the sea. |
| 53 | Swylce geac monað geomran reorde, | Likewise the cuckoo urges with mournful voice, |
| 54 | singeð sumeres weard, sorge beodeð | the guardian of summer sings, announces sorrow, |
| 55 | bitter in breosthord. þæt se beorn ne wat, | bitter in the heart's treasury. The prosperous man |
| 56 | esteadig secg, hwæt þa sume dreogað | does not know what those endure |
| 57 | þe þa wræclastas widost lecgað. | who tread the paths of exile most widely. |
| 58 | Forþon nu min hyge hweorfeð ofer hreþerlocan, | Therefore now my thought roams beyond the confines of my heart, |
| 59 | min modsefa mid mereflode | my spirit, with the sea's flood, |
| 60 | ofer hwæles eþel hweorfeð wide, | travels widely over the whale's homeland, |
| 61 | eorþan sceatas, cymeð eft to me | the earth's expanses; it comes back again to me |
| 62 | gifre ond grædig, gielleð anfloga, | eager and greedy; the lone-flier cries out, |
| 63 | hweteð on hwælweg hreþer unwearnum | urges my heart irresistibly onto the whale's road, |
| 64 | ofer holma gelagu. Forþon me hatran sind | over the expanse of the seas. Therefore warmer to me are |
| 65 | dryhtnes dreamas þonne þis deade lif, | the joys of the Lord than this dead life, |
| 66 | læne on londe. Ic gelyfe no | fleeting on land. I do not believe |
| 67 | þæt him eorðwelan ece stondað. | that earthly riches will stand eternal for him. |
| 68 | Simle þreora sum þinga gehwylce | Always one of three things, in every case, |
| 69 | ær his tid aga, to tweon weorþeð: | before his appointed time, becomes uncertain: |
| 70 | adl oþþe yldo oþþe ecghete | illness, or old age, or the sword's violence |
| 71 | fægum fromweardum feorh oðþringeð. | wrests life away from the one fated and about to depart. |
| 72 | Forþon þæt bið eorla gehwam æftercweþendra | Therefore for every man, the praise of those who live after him, |
| 73 | lof lifgendra lastworda betst, | the best of memorial words, |
| 74 | þæt he gewyrce, ær he on weg scyle, | is that he should achieve, before he must go on his way, |
| 75 | fremum on foldan wið feonda niþ, | through good deeds on earth against the malice of enemies, |
| 76 | deorum dædum deofle togeanes, | through brave deeds, against the devil, |
| 77 | þæt hine ælda bearn æfter hergen, | so that the children of men will praise him afterward, |
| 78 | ond his lof siþþan lifge mid englum | and his fame will then live on with the angels |
| 79 | awa to ealdre, ecan lifes blæd, | forever, the glory of eternal life, |
| 80 | dream mid dugeþum. Dagas sind gewitene, | joy among the hosts. The days have departed, |
| 81 | ealle onmedlan eorþan rices; | all the pomp of earthly kingdoms; |
| 82 | næron nu cyningas ne caseras | there are now no kings, nor emperors, |
| 83 | ne goldgiefan swylce iu wæron, | nor gold-givers such as there once were, |
| 84 | þonne hi mæst mid him mærþa gefremedon | when they performed the greatest glorious deeds among themselves |
| 85 | ond on dryhtlicestum dome lifdon. | and lived in the most lordly splendor. |
| 86 | Gedroren is þeos duguð eal, dreamas sind gewitene, | All that company has fallen, the joys have departed, |
| 87 | wuniað þa wacran ond þas woruld healdaþ, | the weaker ones remain and hold this world, |
| 88 | brucað þurh bisgo. Blæd is gehnæged, | enjoy it through toil. Glory is brought low, |
| 89 | eorþan indryhto ealdað ond searað, | the earth's nobility ages and withers, |
| 90 | swa nu monna gehwylc geond middangeard. | as now does every man throughout middle-earth. |
| 91 | Yldo him on fareð, onsyn blacað, | Old age comes upon him, his face grows pale, |
| 92 | gomelfeax gnornað, wat his iuwine, | the grey-haired man mourns, knows that his old friends, |
| 93 | æþelinga bearn, eorþan forgiefene. | sons of princes, have been given to the earth. |
| 94 | Ne mæg him þonne se flæschoma, þonne him þæt feorg losað, | Nor can his body, when life fails him, |
| 95 | ne swete forswelgan ne sar gefelan, | taste sweetness, nor feel pain, |
| 96 | ne hond onhreran ne mid hyge þencan. | nor move a hand, nor think with his mind. |
| 97 | þeah þe græf wille golde stregan | Though a brother may wish to strew with gold |
| 98 | broþor his geborenum, byrgan be deadum, | the grave of his own kin, bury him among the dead |
| 99 | maþmum mislicum þæt hine mid wille, | with various treasures to go with him, |
| 100 | ne mæg þære sawle þe biþ synna ful | it cannot help the soul which is full of sins — |
| 101 | gold to geoce for godes egsan, | gold, as a remedy against God's terror — |
| 102 | þonne he hit ær hydeð þenden he her leofað. | when he hid it beforehand while he lived here. |
| 103 | Micel biþ se meotudes egsa, forþon hi seo molde oncyrreð; | Great is the terror of the Measurer, by which the earth turns; |
| 104 | se gestaþelade stiþe grundas, | he established the firm foundations, |
| 105 | eorþan sceatas ond uprodor. | the earth's expanses and the heaven above. |
| 106 | Dol biþ se þe him his dryhten ne ondrædeþ; cymeð him se deað unþinged. | Foolish is he who does not fear his Lord; death comes upon him unexpected. |
| 107 | Eadig bið se þe eaþmod leofaþ; cymeð him seo ar of heofonum, | Blessed is he who lives humbly; grace comes to him from the heavens; |
| 108 | meotod him þæt mod gestaþelað, forþon he in his meahte gelyfeð. | the Measurer steadies his spirit, because he trusts in God's might. |
| 109 | Stieran mon sceal strongum mode, ond þæt on staþelum healdan, | A man must steer himself with a strong mind, and keep that steadfast, |
| 110 | ond gewis werum, wisum clæne, | and faithful to men, pure in his ways; |
| 111 | scyle monna gehwylc mid gemete healdan | every man must observe due measure |
| 112 | wiþ leofne ond wið laþne bealo, | in showing hostility toward friend and foe, |
| 113 | þeah þe he hine wille fyres fulne | even though he might wish him engulfed in fire, |
| 114 | oþþe on bæle forbærnedne | or burned on a pyre — |
| 115 | his geworhtne wine. Wyrd biþ swiþre, | his own sworn friend. Fate is stronger, |
| 116 | meotud meahtigra þonne ænges monnes gehygd. | the Measurer mightier, than any man's thought. |
| 117 | Uton we hycgan hwær we ham agen, | Let us consider where we have our true home, |
| 118 | ond þonne geþencan hu we þider cumen, | and then think how we might get there, |
| 119 | ond we þonne eac tilien, þæt we to moten | and let us also strive, that we might enter |
| 120 | in þa ecan eadignesse, | into that eternal blessedness, |
| 121 | þær is lif gelong in lufan dryhtnes, | where life belongs, in the love of the Lord, |
| 122 | hyht in heofonum. þæs sy þam halgan þonc, | hope in the heavens. For that, thanks be to the Holy One, |
| 123 | þæt he usic geweorþade, wuldres ealdor, | that he honored us, the Prince of glory, |
| 124 | ece dryhten, in ealle tid. Amen. | the eternal Lord, for all time. Amen. |
