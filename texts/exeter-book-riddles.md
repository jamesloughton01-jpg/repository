# Exeter Book Riddles (Selection)

The Exeter Book contains roughly 95 riddles (scholars disagree on exact
divisions), most without their solutions recorded — working them out is
part of the point. This is a curated selection of 15 of the most famous
and confidently-solved riddles, rather than the full collection, which
runs to well over a thousand lines and includes many fragmentary or
disputed pieces. Solutions are given after each riddle; try guessing
before reading them. Old English text: sacred-texts.com, Cook & Tinker,
*Select Translations from Old English Poetry* (1902), public domain.
Modern English translation: own work for this repository, prose rendering
(not verse). Numbering follows this source; other editions (Krapp-Dobbie,
Williamson, Muir) number riddles differently.

## Riddle 1 — Storm

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hwylc is hæleþa þæs horsc ond þæs hygecræftig | Who is a person so shrewd and so skilled in mind |
| 2 | þæt þæt mæge asecgan, hwa mec on sið wræce, | that he can say who drives me on my journey, |
| 3 | þonne ic astige strong, stundum reþe, | when I rise up strong, at times fierce, |
| 4 | þrymful þunie, þragum wræce | roar mightily, rage for a time, |
| 5 | fere geond foldan, folcsalo bærne, | travel across the land, burn dwellings, |
| 6 | ræced reafige? Recas stigaþ, | ravage halls? Smoke rises, |
| 7 | haswe ofer hrofum. Hlin biþ on eorþan, | grey over the roofs. There is uproar on the earth, |
| 8 | wælcwealm wera, þonne ic wudu hrere, | deadly slaughter of men, when I shake the woods, |
| 9 | bearwas bledhwate, beamas fylle, | groves rich with fruit, fell the trees, |
| 10 | holme gehrefed, heahum meahtum | roofed by the sea, driven by lofty powers, |
| 11 | wrecen on waþe, wide sended; | driven on my way, sent far and wide; |
| 12 | hæbbe me on hrycge þæt ær hadas wreah | I carry on my back what once covered the forms |
| 13 | foldbuendra, flæsc ond gæstas, | of earth-dwellers, flesh and spirits, |
| 14 | somod on sunde. Saga hwa mec þecce, | together in the flood. Say who covers me, |
| 15 | oþþe hu ic hatte, þe þa hlæst bere. | or what I am called, who bears that burden. |

**Solution:** Storm / wind (one of the more contested riddles — some scholars propose other readings).

## Riddle 2 — The Sea

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hwilum ic gewite, swa ne wenaþ men, | At times I depart, as men do not expect, |
| 2 | under yþa geþræc eorþan secan, | to seek the earth beneath the tumult of the waves, |
| 3 | garsecges grund. Gifen biþ gewreged, | the depths of the ocean. The sea is stirred up, |
| 4 | fam gewealcen; | foam tossed about; |
| 5 | hwælmere hlimmeð, hlude grimmeð, | the whale-mere roars, roars loudly, |
| 6 | streamas staþu beataþ, stundum weorpaþ | the currents beat the shores, at times hurl |
| 7 | on stealc hleoþa stane ond sonde, | onto the steep cliffs stone and sand, |
| 8 | ware ond wæge, þonne ic winnende, | seaweed and wave, when I, struggling, |
| 9 | holmmægne biþeaht, hrusan styrge, | covered by the sea's might, stir up the earth, |
| 10 | side sægrundas. Sundhelme ne mæg | the wide sea-bottoms. I cannot escape my sea-covering |
| 11 | losian ær mec læte se þe min latteow biþ | before he who is my guide releases me |
| 12 | on siþa gehwam. Saga, þoncol mon, | on each of my journeys. Say, thoughtful person, |
| 13 | hwa mec bregde of brimes fæþmum, | who draws me from the sea's embrace, |
| 14 | þonne streamas eft stille weorþaþ, | when the currents again grow still, |
| 15 | yþa geþwære, þe mec ær wrugon. | the waves calm, that had before covered me. |

**Solution:** The sea / tides (a companion piece to Riddle 1).

## Riddle 5 — Shield

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic eom anhaga, iserne wund, | I am a solitary one, wounded by iron, |
| 2 | bille gebennad, beadoweorca sæd, | scarred by the sword, weary of battles, |
| 3 | ecgum werig. Oft ic wig seo, | worn by blade-edges. Often I look upon war, |
| 4 | frecne feohtan. Frofre ne wene, | dangerous fighting. I do not expect comfort, |
| 5 | þæt me geoc cyme guðgewinnes, | that relief will come to me from the battle-strife, |
| 6 | ær ic mid ældum eal forwurðe, | before I perish utterly among men; |
| 7 | ac mec hnossiaþ homera lafe, | but the leavings of hammers strike me, |
| 8 | heardecg heoroscearp, hondweorc smiþa, | hard-edged and sword-sharp, the handiwork of smiths, |
| 9 | bitaþ in burgum; ic abidan sceal | bite into me in the strongholds; I must await |
| 10 | laþran gemotes. Næfre læcecynn | a more hateful encounter. Never could I find |
| 11 | on folcstede findan meahte, | in any dwelling-place a kind of physician |
| 12 | þara þe mid wyrtum wunde gehælde, | who might heal my wound with herbs, |
| 13 | ac me ecga dolg eacen weorðaþ | but the scar of blades grows greater on me |
| 14 | þurh deaðslege dagum ond nihtum. | through deadly blows, by day and by night. |

**Solution:** Shield.

## Riddle 7 — Swan

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hrægl min swigaþ, þonne ic hrusan trede, | My garment is silent, when I tread the earth, |
| 2 | oþþe þa wic buge, oþþe wado drefe. | or dwell in my abode, or stir the waters. |
| 3 | Hwilum mec ahebbaþ ofer hæleþa byht | At times my trappings and this high air |
| 4 | hyrste mine, ond þeos hea lyft, | lift me up over the dwellings of men, |
| 5 | ond mec þonne wide wolcna strengu | and then the strength of the clouds carries me |
| 6 | ofer folc byreþ. Fræwe mine | far over the people. My adornments |
| 7 | swogaþ hlude ond swinsiaþ, | resound loudly and make music, |
| 8 | torhte singaþ, þonne ic getenge ne beom | sing clearly, when I am not touching |
| 9 | flode ond foldan, ferende gæst. | the flood and the earth — a traveling spirit. |

**Solution:** Swan (the sound of its wings in flight).

## Riddle 9 — Cuckoo

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mec on þissum dagum deadne ofgeafun | In these days, my father and mother |
| 2 | fæder ond modor; ne wæs me feorh þa gen, | abandoned me for dead; there was not yet life in me, |
| 3 | ealdor in innan. Þa mec an ongon, | nor spirit within. Then a certain kinswoman, |
| 4 | welhold mege, wedum þeccan, | entirely loyal, began to cover me with garments, |
| 5 | heold ond freoþode, hleosceorpe wrah | kept and protected me, wrapped me in a sheltering robe |
| 6 | swa arlice swa hire agen bearn, | just as kindly as her own child, |
| 7 | oþþæt ic under sceate, swa min gesceapu wæron, | until, under that covering, as was my nature, |
| 8 | ungesibbum wearð eacen gæste. | I grew, endowed with spirit, among those not of my kin. |
| 9 | Mec seo friþe mæg fedde siþþan, | That protecting kinswoman fed me afterward, |
| 10 | oþþæt ic aweox, widdor meahte | until I grew up, and could set out |
| 11 | siþas asettan. Heo hæfde swæsra þy læs | more widely on my travels. She had fewer |
| 12 | suna ond dohtra, þy heo swa dyde. | of her own dear children, because she did so. |

**Solution:** Cuckoo (brood parasitism — laying eggs in another bird's nest).

## Riddle 20 — Sword

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic eom wunderlicu wiht, on gewin sceapen, | I am a wondrous creature, shaped for battle, |
| 2 | frean minum leof, fægre gegyrwed. | dear to my lord, fairly adorned. |
| 3 | Byrne is min bleofag, swylce beorht seomað | My mail-coat is many-colored, and bright wire |
| 4 | wir ymb þone wælgim þe me waldend geaf, | lies coiled around the deadly gem my ruler gave me, |
| 5 | se me widgalum wisaþ hwilum | who guides me, far-ranging, at times |
| 6 | sylfum to sace. Þonne ic sinc wege | to combat myself. Then I carry treasure |
| 7 | þurh hlutterne dæg, hondweorc smiþa, | through the bright day, the handiwork of smiths, |
| 8 | gold ofer geardas. Oft ic gæstberend | gold across the courtyards. Often I kill |
| 9 | cwelle compwæpnum. Cyning mec gyrweþ | life-bearers with weapons of war. A king adorns me |
| 10 | since ond seolfre ond mec on sele weorþaþ; | with treasure and silver, honors me in the hall; |
| 11 | ne wyrneþ wordlofes, wisan mæneþ | he does not withhold praise, speaks of my qualities |
| 12 | mine for mengo, þær hy meodu drincaþ, | before the company, where they drink mead; |
| 13 | healdeþ mec on heaþore, hwilum læteþ eft | he keeps me restrained, at times lets me again, |
| 14 | radwerigne on gerum sceacan, | weary from the road, range freely, |
| 15 | orlegfromne. Oft ic oþrum scod | eager for battle. Often I have hurt another, |
| 16 | frecne æt his freonde; fah eom ic wide, | dangerously, at his friend's side; I am hostile far and wide, |
| 17 | wæpnum awyrged. Ic me wenan ne þearf | accursed among weapons. I need not expect |
| 18 | þæt me bearn wræce on bonan feore, | that a son will avenge me on my slayer's life, |
| 19 | gif me gromra hwylc guþe genægeþ; | if any enemy attacks me in battle; |
| 20 | ne weorþeþ sio mægburg gemicledu | nor will that family be increased |
| 21 | eaforan minum þe ic æfter woc, | by offspring of mine, whom I might beget — |
| 22 | nymþe ic hlafordleas hweorfan mote | unless I, lordless, may turn away |
| 23 | from þam healdende þe me hringas geaf. | from the keeper who gave me rings. |
| 24 | Me bið forð witod, gif ic frean hyre, | It is destined for me, if I obey my lord, |
| 25 | guþe fremme, swa ic gien dyde | wage war, as I have done before |
| 26 | minum þeodne on þonc, þæt ic þolian sceal | to my prince's satisfaction, that I must forgo |
| 27 | bearngestreona. Ic wið bryde ne mot | begetting children. I may not have |
| 28 | hæmed habban, ac me þæs hyhtplegan | intercourse with a bride; that joyful play |
| 29 | geno wyrneð, se mec geara on | is still forbidden me, by him who long ago |
| 30 | bende legde; forþon ic brucan sceal | laid bonds upon me; therefore I must, unmarried, |
| 31 | on hagostealde hæleþa gestreona. | enjoy the treasures of warriors. |
| 32 | Oft ic wirum dol wife abelge, | Often, foolish with ornaments, I anger a woman, |
| 33 | wonie hyre willan; heo me wom spreceð, | diminish her desire; she speaks ill of me, |
| 34 | floceþ hyre folmum, firenaþ mec wordum, | claps her hands, reproaches me with words, |
| 35 | ungod gæleð. Ic ne gyme þæs compes. | utters unkindness. I care nothing for that contest. |

**Solution:** Sword (one of the riddles with well-known sexual double meaning alongside its literal one).

## Riddle 21 — Plough

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Neb is min niþerweard; neol ic fere | My nose points downward; I travel bowed low |
| 2 | ond be grunde græfe, geonge swa me wisaþ | and scrape along the ground, moving as guides me |
| 3 | har holtes feond, ond hlaford min | the grey enemy of the forest, and my lord |
| 4 | woh færeþ weard æt steorte, | walks bent over, a guardian at my tail, |
| 5 | wrigaþ on wonge, wegeþ mec ond þyð, | strides across the field, carries and pushes me, |
| 6 | saweþ on swæð min. Ic snyþige forð, | sows in my track. I rush onward, |
| 7 | brungen of bearwe, bunden cræfte, | brought from a grove, cunningly bound, |
| 8 | wegen on wægne, hæbbe wundra fela; | carried on a wagon; I possess many wonders. |
| 9 | me biþ gongendre grene on healfe | As I go, green is on one side of me, |
| 10 | ond min swæð sweotol sweart on oþre. | and my track is clearly black on the other. |
| 11 | Me þurh hrycg wrecen hongaþ under | Through my back is thrust, hanging below, |
| 12 | an orþoncpil, oþer on heafde, | one skillfully-made point, another on my head, |
| 13 | fæst ond forðweard. Fealleþ on sidan | fixed and forward-pointing. What I tear with my teeth |
| 14 | þæt ic toþum tere, gif me teala þenaþ | falls to the side, if he who stands behind me |
| 15 | hindeweardre, þæt bið hlaford min. | serves me rightly — that is my lord. |

**Solution:** Plough.

## Riddle 25 — Onion

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic eom wunderlicu wiht, wifum on hyhte, | I am a wondrous creature, a joy to women, |
| 2 | neahbuendum nyt; nængum sceþþe | useful to neighbors; I harm none |
| 3 | burgsittendra, nymþe bonan anum. | of the town-dwellers, except my slayer alone. |
| 4 | Staðol min is steapheah, stonde ic on bedde, | My base stands high; I stand in a bed, |
| 5 | neoþan ruh nathwær. Neþeð hwilum | shaggy somewhere below. Sometimes |
| 6 | ful cyrtenu ceorles dohtor, | a very pretty peasant's daughter dares, |
| 7 | modwlonc meowle, þæt heo on mec gripeð, | a proud-hearted woman, to grab hold of me, |
| 8 | ræseð mec on reodne, reafað min heafod, | rushes at me when reddened, plunders my head, |
| 9 | fegeð mec on fæsten. Feleþ sona | and fastens me into a confined place. She feels at once |
| 10 | mines gemotes, seo þe mec nearwaþ, | my meeting, she who confines me, |
| 11 | wif wundenlocc. Wæt bið þæt eage. | a woman with braided hair. Wet will be that eye. |

**Solution:** Onion (famous for its bawdy double meaning).

## Riddle 26 — Bible (Gospel Book)

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mec feonda sum feore besnyþede, | A certain enemy robbed me of my life, |
| 2 | woruldstrenga binom, wætte siþþan, | took away my worldly strength; then wetted me, |
| 3 | dyfde on wætre, dyde eft þonan, | dipped me in water, took me out again, |
| 4 | sette on sunnan, þær ic swiþe beleas | set me in the sun, where I quickly lost |
| 5 | herum þam þe ic hæfde. Heard mec siþþan | the hairs I had. The hard edge of a knife, |
| 6 | snað seaxses ecg, sindrum begrunden; | ground free of impurities, then cut me; |
| 7 | fingras feoldan, ond mec fugles wyn | fingers folded me, and the joy of a bird [a quill] |
| 8 | geond speddropum spyrede geneahhe, | traced over me often, with its drops of ink, |
| 9 | ofer brunne brerd, beamtelge swealg, | across the dark border, swallowed tree-dye, |
| 10 | streames dæle, stop eft on mec, | a portion of liquid, stepped upon me again, |
| 11 | siþade sweartlast. Mec siþþan wrah | traveled a dark track. A man then covered me |
| 12 | hæleð hleobordum, hyde beþenede, | with protective boards, stretched a hide over me, |
| 13 | gierede mec mid golde; forþon me gliwedon | adorned me with gold; therefore the wondrous work |
| 14 | wrætlic weorc smiþa, wire bifongen. | of smiths, wire-encircled, glorified me. |
| 15 | Nu þa gereno ond se reada telg | Now those ornaments and the red dye |
| 16 | ond þa wuldorgesteald wide mære | and those glorious furnishings, widely famed, |
| 17 | dryhtfolca helm, nales dol wite. | honor me — protector of the people — not foolish torment. |
| 18 | Gif min bearn wera brucan willað, | If the children of men wish to make use of me, |
| 19 | hy beoð þy gesundran ond þy sigefæstran, | they will be the sounder for it, and more victorious, |
| 20 | heortum þy hwætran ond þy hygebliþran, | bolder in heart, and more joyful in mind, |
| 21 | ferþe þy frodran, habbað freonda þy ma, | wiser in spirit; they will have more friends, |
| 22 | swæsra ond gesibbra, soþra ond godra, | dearer and closer kin, truer and better, |
| 23 | tilra ond getreowra, þa hyra tyr ond ead | more excellent and more faithful, who will graciously |
| 24 | estum ycað ond hy arstafum | increase their honor and prosperity, and surround them |
| 25 | lissum bilecgaþ ond hi lufan fæþmum | with kindness and favor, and clasp them fast |
| 26 | fæste clyppað. Frige hwæt ic hatte, | in the embrace of love. Ask what I am called, |
| 27 | niþum to nytte. Nama min is mære, | useful to men. My name is famous, |
| 28 | hæleþum gifre ond halig sylf. | beneficial to men, and holy in itself. |

**Solution:** Bible / Gospel book (describing vellum-making: skin, soaking, drying, cutting, writing, gold-decorated binding).

## Riddle 30 — Wood (Beam)

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic eom legbysig, lace mid winde, | I am busy with flame, I play with the wind, |
| 2 | bewunden mid wuldre, wedre gesomnad, | wrapped about with glory, joined with the weather, |
| 3 | fus forðweges, fyre gebysgad, | eager for my forth-going, troubled by fire, |
| 4 | bearu blowende, byrnende gled. | a flowering grove, a burning ember. |
| 5 | Ful oft mec gesiþas sendaþ æfter hondum, | Very often companions send me from hand to hand, |
| 6 | þæt mec weras ond wif wlonce cyssaþ. | so that proud men and women kiss me. |
| 7 | Þonne ic mec onhæbbe, ond hi onhnigaþ to me | When I raise myself up, and many bow down to me |

| 9 | ycan upcyme eadignesse. | increase the rising of blessedness. |

## Riddle 33 — Iceberg

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wiht cwom æfter wege wrætlicu liþan, | A creature came traveling, wondrous, over the wave, |
| 2 | cymlic from ceole cleopode to londe, | beautiful, called out to the land from its vessel, |
| 3 | hlinsade hlude; hleahtor wæs gryrelic, | resounded loudly; its laughter was terrible, |
| 4 | egesful on earde, ecge wæron scearpe. | fearsome in the land, its edges were sharp. |
| 5 | Wæs hio hetegrim, hilde to sæne, | It was fierce and hostile, slow to battle, |
| 6 | biter beadoweorca; bordweallas grof, | bitter in deeds of war; it carved through shield-walls, |
| 7 | heardhiþende. Heterune bond, | a harsh ravager. It bound a rune of hostility, |
| 8 | sægde searocræftig ymb hyre sylfre gesceaft: | spoke cunningly about its own origin: |
| 9 | "Is min modor mægða cynnes | "My mother is of the dearest kind |
| 10 | þæs deorestan, þæt is dohtor min | of maidens, so that she is my daughter, |
| 11 | eacen up liden, swa þæt is ældum cuð, | grown and risen up, as is known to men, |
| 12 | firum on folce, þæt seo on foldan sceal | to people among the folk, that she must stand, |
| 13 | on ealra londa gehwam lissum stondan." | on the earth, in every land, with grace." |

**Solution:** Iceberg (a paradox riddle: ice comes from water, its "mother," and melts back into water, its "daughter").

## Riddle 35 — Mail-coat

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mec se wæta wong, wundrum freorig, | The wet plain, wondrously cold, |
| 2 | of his innaþe ærist cende. | first brought me forth from its womb. |
| 3 | Ne wat ic mec beworhtne wulle flysum, | I do not know myself to be made of wool fleece, |
| 4 | hærum þurh heahcræft, hygeþoncum min. | of hairs, through high skill, by my own thoughts. |
| 5 | Wundene me ne beoð wefle, ne ic wearp hafu, | No woven threads are in me, nor do I have a warp; |
| 6 | ne þurh þreata geþræcu þræd me ne hlimmeð, | nor through the crowding of throngs does a thread resound in me, |
| 7 | ne æt me hrutende hrisil scriþeð, | nor does a whirring shuttle glide over me, |
| 8 | ne mec ohwonan sceal am cnyssan. | nor must a beater strike me from anywhere. |
| 9 | Wyrmas mec ne awæfan wyrda cræftum, | Worms did not weave me with the skills of fate, |
| 10 | þa þe geolo godwebb geatwum fræwaþ. | those that adorn yellow, precious cloth with ornament. |
| 11 | Wile mec mon hwæþre seþeah wide ofer eorþan | Yet nevertheless men will, widely over the earth, |
| 12 | hatan for hæleþum hyhtlic gewæde. | call me a joyful garment for warriors. |
| 13 | Saga soðcwidum, searoþoncum gleaw, | Say, with true words, skilled in cunning thoughts, |
| 14 | wordum wisfæst, hwæt þis gewæde sy. | wise in speech, what this garment may be. |

**Solution:** Mail-coat / chain mail (its "wool" is iron ore drawn from the earth, not woven by conventional means).

## Riddle 43 — Soul and Body

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic wat indryhtne æþelum deorne | I know a noble guest, dear in his nature, |
| 2 | giest in geardum, þam se grimma ne mæg | in the dwellings, whom fierce hunger |
| 3 | hungor sceððan ne se hata þurst, | cannot harm, nor hot thirst, |
| 4 | yldo ne adle. Gif him arlice | nor age, nor sickness. If a servant |
| 5 | esne þenaþ, se þe agan sceal | honorably attends him — one who must accompany him |
| 6 | on þam siðfate, hy gesunde æt ham | on that journey — they will find, safe at home, |
| 7 | findaþ witode him wiste ond blisse, | provided for them, feasting and bliss, |
| 8 | cnosles unrim, care, gif se esne | countless kin; but sorrow, if the servant |
| 9 | his hlaforde hyreð yfle, | obeys his lord badly, |
| 10 | frean on fore. Ne wile forht wesan | his master, on the way. Brother will not wish |
| 11 | broþor oþrum; him þæt bam sceðeð, | to be fearful toward brother; that harms them both, |
| 12 | þonne hy from bearme begen hweorfaþ | when they both must depart, eager to go elsewhere, |
| 13 | anre magan ellorfuse, | from the bosom of one kinswoman — |
| 14 | moddor ond sweostor. Mon, se þe wille, | mother and sister. Whoever wishes may |
| 15 | cyþe cynewordum hu se cuma hatte, | declare, in fitting words, what the guest is called, |
| 16 | eðþa se esne, þe ic her ymb sprice. | or the servant, of whom I speak here. |

**Solution:** Soul and Body (the "guest" is the soul, the "servant" the body).

## Riddle 47 — Bookworm

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Moððe word fræt. Me þæt þuhte | A moth ate words. That seemed to me |
| 2 | wrætlicu wyrd, þa ic þæt wundor gefrægn, | a strange fate, when I heard of that wonder — |
| 3 | þæt se wyrm forswealg wera gied sumes, | that the worm had swallowed a certain man's song, |
| 4 | þeof in þystro, þrymfæstne cwide | a thief in the darkness, a glorious utterance |
| 5 | ond þæs strangan staþol. Stælgiest ne wæs | and its strong foundation. The thieving guest was |
| 6 | wihte þy gleawra, þe he þam wordum swealg. | not at all the wiser for it, though he swallowed those words. |

**Solution:** Bookworm (a moth larva eating a manuscript).

## Riddle 60 — Reed

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic wæs be sonde, sæwealle neah, | I was by the sand, near the sea-wall, |
| 2 | æt merefaroðe, minum gewunade | at the ocean-shore; I dwelt, fixed |
| 3 | frumstaþole fæst; fea ænig wæs | in my first foundation. Few indeed there were, |
| 4 | monna cynnes, þæt minne þær | of the race of men, who beheld my dwelling |
| 5 | on anæde eard beheolde, | there in that solitary place, |
| 6 | ac mec uhtna gehwam yð sio brune | but each dawn the dark wave |

| 13 | eorles ingeþonc ond ord somod, | a man's inner thought together with the point, |

**Solution:** Reed, cut into a pen (a reed grows by the shore, then is cut with a knife into a quill for private written messages — thematically linked to the poem that follows it, *The Husband's Message*).
