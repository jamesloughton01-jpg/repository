# Beowulf

**Partial excerpt** — Beowulf is 3,182 lines in total; this collection
includes only the opening (the Prelude and Fitts I-III, lines 1-257: the
funeral of Scyld Scefing, the building of Heorot, Grendel's attacks, and
the start of Beowulf's voyage to Denmark). It is not being extended
further, to avoid assembling a complete alternative translation of the
single most-translated work in this collection. See the source edition
linked below for the rest of the poem.

Composed by an unknown author at an uncertain date (estimates range from
the 8th to the early 11th century); survives in a single manuscript,
Cotton Vitellius A.xv (British Library), copied c. 1000 AD. The
foundational epic of Old English literature: the hero Beowulf's fights
against the monster Grendel, Grendel's mother, and, decades later, a
dragon. Old English text: Wikisource, *Beowulf (Wyatt)* — A.J. Wyatt's
1894 edition based on Julius Zupitza's 1882 facsimile of the manuscript,
public domain (published before 1931). Modern English translation: own
work for this repository, prose rendering (not verse).

## Prelude — The Funeral of Scyld Scefing

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hwæt! we Gar-Dena in geardagum, | Listen! We have heard of the glory, in days gone by, |
| 2 | þeodcyninga þrym gefrunon, | of the Spear-Danes' kings, |
| 3 | hu ða æþelingas ellen fremedon. | how those princes performed brave deeds. |
| 4 | Oft Scyld Scefing sceaþena þreatum, | Often Scyld Scefing seized the mead-benches |
| 5 | monegum mægþum meodosetla ofteah, | from bands of enemies, from many tribes; |
| 6 | egsode eorl, syððan ærest wearð | he terrified their warriors, though he had first |
| 7 | feasceaft funden; he þæs frofre gebad, | been found destitute; he lived to see comfort for that, |
| 8 | weox under wolcnum, weorðmyndum þah, | grew up under the skies, prospered in honors, |
| 9 | oð þæt him æghwylc þara ymbsittendra | until every one of those who lived around him, |
| 10 | ofer hronrade hyran scolde, | across the whale-road, had to obey him, |
| 11 | gomban gyldan; þæt wæs god cyning. | pay him tribute; that was a good king! |
| 12 | Ðæm eafera wæs æfter cenned, | To him an heir was afterward born, |
| 13 | geong in geardum, þone God sende | young in his household, whom God sent |
| 14 | folce to frofre; fyrenðearfe ongeat | as a comfort to the people; he had perceived the dire distress |
| 15 | þæt hie ær drugon aldorlease | that they had suffered before, lordless, |
| 16 | lange hwile. Him þæs Liffrea, | for a long time. Therefore the Lord of Life, |
| 17 | wuldres Wealdend, woroldare forgeaf; | the Ruler of glory, granted him honor in the world; |
| 18 | Beowulf wæs breme (blæd wide sprang), | Beow was renowned — his fame spread widely — |
| 19 | Scyldes eafera, Scedelandum in. | Scyld's heir, throughout the Danish lands. |
| 20 | Swa sceal geong guma gode gewyrcean, | So must a young man bring it about, by good deeds, |
| 21 | fromum feohgiftum on fæder wine, | by generous gifts of treasure while still in his father's keeping, |
| 22 | þæt hine on ylde eft gewunigen | that later in life, in turn, |
| 23 | wilgesiþas, þonne wig cume, | beloved companions will stand by him when war comes, |
| 24 | leode gelæsten; lofdædum sceal | will serve their lord; by praiseworthy deeds |
| 25 | in mægþa gehwære man geþeon. | a man shall prosper among every people. |
| 26 | Him ða Scyld gewat to gescæphwile | Then Scyld departed, at his appointed hour, |
| 27 | felahror feran on frean wære; | still very vigorous, to go into the Lord's keeping; |
| 28 | hi hyne þa ætbæron to brimes faroðe, | they then carried him out to the sea's current, |
| 29 | swæse gesiþas, swa he selfa bæd, | his dear companions, just as he himself had asked, |
| 30 | þenden wordum weold wine Scyldinga, | while he, the friend of the Scyldings, still ruled with his words — |
| 31 | leof landfruma lange ahte. | that beloved land-prince had long possessed it. |
| 32 | Þær æt hyðe stod hringedstefna, | There at the harbor stood the ring-prowed ship, |
| 33 | isig ond utfus, æþelinges fær; | icy and eager to depart, a vessel fit for a prince; |
| 34 | aledon þa leofne þeoden, | they laid down their beloved lord, |
| 35 | beaga bryttan, on bearm scipes, | the giver of rings, in the bosom of the ship, |
| 36 | mærne be mæste. Þær wæs madma fela, | the glorious one, by the mast. There was a great store of treasures, |
| 37 | of feorwegum, frætwa gelæded. | ornaments brought from distant lands. |
| 38 | Ne hyrde ic cymlicor ceol gegyrwan | I have never heard of a ship more splendidly equipped |
| 39 | hildewæpnum ond heaðowædum, | with war-weapons and battle-garments, |
| 40 | billum ond byrnum; him on bearme læg | swords and mail-coats; upon his breast lay |
| 41 | madma mænigo, þa him mid scoldon | a multitude of treasures, which were to journey with him |
| 42 | on flodes æht feor gewitan. | far away into the sea's possession. |
| 43 | Nalæs hi hine læssan lacum teodan, | They provided him with no lesser gifts, |
| 44 | þeodgestreonum, þonne þa dydon, | no fewer national treasures, than those |
| 45 | þe hine æt frumsceafte forð onsendon | who at his beginning had sent him forth |
| 46 | ænne ofer yðe umborwesende. | alone over the waves, a mere child. |
| 47 | Þa gyt hie him asetton segen gyldenne | Then further they set up a golden standard |
| 48 | heah ofer heafod, leton holm beran, | high above his head, let the sea carry him, |
| 49 | geafon on garsecg; him wæs geomor sefa, | gave him to the ocean; their spirit was sorrowful, |
| 50 | murnende mod. Men ne cunnon | their mood mourning. Men cannot say for certain — |
| 51 | secgan to soðe, selerædende, | neither counselors in hall |
| 52 | hæleð under heofenum, hwa þæm hlæste onfeng. | nor warriors under the heavens — who received that cargo. |

## Fitt I — The Building of Heorot

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 53 | Ða wæs on burgum Beowulf Scyldinga, | Then was Beow of the Scyldings, |
| 54 | leof leodcyning, longe þrage | beloved king of the people, renowned among the nations for a long time |
| 55 | folcum gefræge (fæder ellor hwearf, | (his father had passed away, |
| 56 | aldor of earde), oþ þæt him eft onwoc | the prince from his home), until in turn there was born to him |
| 57 | heah Healfdene; heold þenden lifde, | great Healfdene; he ruled, while he lived, |
| 58 | gamol ond guðreouw, glæde Scyldingas. | aged and fierce in war, the gracious Scyldings. |
| 59 | Ðæm feower bearn forð gerimed | To him four children, reckoned in order, |
| 60 | in worold wocun, weoroda ræswa | were born into the world, leader of hosts: |
| 61 | Heorogar, ond Hroðgar ond Halga til; | Heorogar, and Hrothgar, and good Halga; |
| 62 | hyrde ic, þæt [Yrse wæs Onelan cwen] | and I have heard that [Yrse was Onela's queen], |
| 63 | Heaðo-Scilfinges healsgebedda. | the bedfellow of the Battle-Scylfing. |
| 64 | Þa wæs Hroðgare heresped gyfen, | Then success in war was granted to Hrothgar, |
| 65 | wiges weorðmynd, þæt him his winemagas | honor in battle, so that his kinsmen and retainers |
| 66 | georne hyrdon, oðð þæt seo geogoð geweox, | eagerly obeyed him, until the young men grew |
| 67 | magodriht micel. Him on mod bearn, | into a great band of warriors. It came into his mind |
| 68 | þæt healreced hatan wolde, | that he would command a hall-building, |
| 69 | medoærn micel men gewyrcean, | order men to construct a great mead-hall, |
| 70 | þon[n]e yldo bearn æfre gefrunon, | greater than the children of men had ever heard of, |
| 71 | ond þær on innan eall gedælan | and there within to distribute everything |
| 72 | geongum ond ealdum, swylc him God sealde, | to young and old, as God had given him, |
| 73 | buton folcscare ond feorum gumena. | except for the common land and the lives of men. |
| 74 | Ða ic wide gefrægn weorc gebannan | Then I have heard widely that the work was proclaimed |
| 75 | manigre mægþe geond þisne middangeard, | to many a tribe throughout this middle-earth, |
| 76 | folcstede frætwan. Him on fyrste gelomp | to adorn the dwelling-place. In time it came to pass, |
| 77 | ædre mid yldum, þæt hit wearð eal gearo, | quickly among men, that it was all made ready, |
| 78 | healærna mæst; scop him Heort naman, | the greatest of hall-buildings; he gave it the name "Heorot," |
| 79 | se þe his wordes geweald wide hæfde. | he whose word held power far and wide. |
| 80 | He beot ne aleh, beagas dælde, | He did not go back on his boast; he distributed rings, |
| 81 | sinc æt symle. Sele hlifade | treasure at the feast. The hall towered up, |
| 82 | heah ond horngeap; heaðowylma bad | high and wide-gabled; it awaited the fierce surges |
| 83 | laðan liges. Ne wæs hit lenge þa gen, | of hostile flame. Nor was it yet long, |
| 84 | þæt se ecghete aþumswerian | until the sword-hatred of son-in-law and father-in-law |
| 85 | æfter wælniðe wæcnan scolde. | should awaken after deadly enmity. |
| 86 | Ða se ellengæst earfoðlice | Then the powerful spirit endured hardship, grievously, |
| 87 | þrage geþolode, se þe in þystrum bad, | for a time — he who dwelt in darkness — |
| 88 | þæt he dogora gehwam dream gehyrde | in that every day he heard revelry |
| 89 | hludne in healle; þær wæs hearpan sweg, | loud in the hall; there was the sound of the harp, |
| 90 | swutol sang scopes. Sægde se þe cuþe | the clear song of the minstrel. He who knew how |
| 91 | frumsceaft fira feorran reccan, | to tell from far back the origin of men spoke, |
| 92 | cwæð þæt se Ælmihtiga eorðan worhte, | said that the Almighty made the earth, |
| 93 | wlitebeorhtne wang, swa wæter bebugeð; | a bright and beautiful plain, as far as the water surrounds it, |
| 94 | gesette sigehreþig sunnan and monan | set the sun and moon, triumphant, |
| 95 | leoman to leohte landbuendum, | as lights to shine for the land-dwellers, |
| 96 | ond gefrætwade foldan sceatas | and adorned the corners of the earth |
| 97 | leomum and leafum; lif eac gesceop | with branches and leaves; he also created life |
| 98 | cynna gehwylcum, þara ðe cwice hwyrfaþ. | for every kind of creature that moves and lives. |
| 99 | Swa ða drihtguman dreamum lifdon | So the retainers lived in joy, |
| 100 | eadiglice, oð ðæt an ongan | blessedly, until one began |
| 101 | fyrene fremman, feond on helle; | to commit crimes, a fiend from hell; |
| 102 | wæs se grimma gæst Grendel haten, | that grim spirit was called Grendel, |
| 103 | mære mearcstapa, se þe moras heold, | the notorious prowler of the borderland, who held the moors, |
| 104 | fen ond fæsten; fifelcynnes eard | the fen and stronghold; the home of the monster-race, |
| 105 | wonsæli wer weardode hwile, | the unhappy creature, had guarded that region for a time, |
| 106 | siþðan him Scyppend forscrifen hæfde. | since the Creator had condemned him. |
| 107 | In Caines cynne þone cwealm gewræc, | The eternal Lord avenged that killing on Cain's kin, |
| 108 | ece Drihten, þæs þe he Abel sloh. | because he slew Abel. |
| 109 | Ne gefeah he þære fæhðe, ac he hine feor forwræc, | He did not profit from that feud, but the Ruler drove him far away, |
| 110 | Metod for þy mane, mancynne fram. | from mankind, on account of that crime. |
| 111 | Ðanon untydras ealle onwocon, | From him all evil offspring were born — |
| 112 | eotenas ond ylfe ond orcneas, | ogres and elves and evil spirits, |
| 113 | swylce gigantas, þa wið Gode wunnon | likewise the giants who struggled against God |
| 114 | lange þrage; he him ðæs lean forgeald. | for a long time; he repaid them for that. |

## Fitt II — Grendel's Attacks Begin

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 115 | Gewat ða neosian, syþðan niht becom, | Then it went to seek out, once night had come, |
| 116 | hean huses, hu hit Hring-Dene | the lofty house, to see how the Ring-Danes |
| 117 | æfter beorþege gebun hæfdon. | had occupied it after their beer-drinking. |
| 118 | Fand þa ðær inne æþelinga gedriht | It found therein a company of nobles |
| 119 | swefan æfter symble; sorge ne cuðon, | asleep after the feast; they knew no sorrow, |
| 120 | wonsceaft wera. Wiht unhælo, | no misery of men. The creature of evil, |
| 121 | grim ond grædig, gearo sona wæs, | grim and greedy, was soon ready, |
| 122 | reoc ond reþe, ond on ræste genam | fierce and cruel, and seized from their rest |
| 123 | þritig þegna; þanon eft gewat | thirty thegns; from there it went back again, |
| 124 | huðe hremig to ham faran, | exulting in its plunder, to travel home, |
| 125 | mid þære wælfylle wica neosan. | to seek its dwelling with that fill of slaughter. |
| 126 | Ða wæs on uhtan mid ærdæge | Then, at dawn, in the early morning, |
| 127 | Grendles guðcræft gumum undyrne; | Grendel's war-strength became known to men; |
| 128 | þa wæs æfter wiste wop up ahafen, | then, after the feasting, weeping was raised up, |
| 129 | micel morgensweg. Mære þeoden, | a great cry in the morning. The famous prince, |
| 130 | æþeling ærgod, unbliðe sæt, | the noble long-excellent, sat joyless; |
| 131 | þolode ðryðswyðe, þegnsorge dreah, | the mighty one suffered, endured grief for his thegns, |
| 132 | syðþan hie þæs laðan last sceawedon, | when they examined the tracks of that hateful one, |
| 133 | wergan gastes; wæs þæt gewin to strang, | that accursed spirit; that struggle was too fierce, |
| 134 | lað ond longsum. Næs hit lengra fyrst, | hateful and long-lasting. Nor was the respite any longer, |
| 135 | ac ymb ane niht eft gefremede | but after one more night he committed |
| 136 | morðbeala mare ond no mearn fore | yet more killings, and did not grieve at all |
| 137 | fæhðe ond fyrene; wæs to fæst on þam. | over that feud and crime — he was too fixed in it. |
| 138 | Þa wæs eaðfynde, þe him elles hwær | Then it was easy to find one who sought rest |
| 139 | gerumlicor ræste sohte, | elsewhere, more comfortably, |
| 140 | bed æfter burum, ða him gebeacnod wæs, | a bed among the outer chambers, once it had been made clear, |
| 141 | gesægd soðlice, sweotolan tacne | plainly declared, with a clear sign, |
| 142 | healðegnes hete; heold hyne syðþan | the hall-thegn's danger; he who escaped the fiend |
| 143 | fyr ond fæstor, se þæm feonde ætwand. | then kept himself further and more safely away. |
| 144 | Swa rixode ond wið rihte wan | So it ruled, and fought against right, |
| 145 | ana wið eallum, oð þæt idel stod | alone against all, until the best of houses |
| 146 | husa selest. Wæs seo hwil micel; | stood empty. That was a long time; |
| 147 | twelf wintra tid torn geþolode | for twelve years' time the friend of the Scyldings |
| 148 | wine Scyldinga, weana gehwelcne, | endured anguish, every kind of woe, |
| 149 | sidra sorga; forðam [syðþan] wearð | vast sorrows; and so it later became |
| 150 | ylda bearnum undyrne cuð, | well known to the children of men, plainly, |
| 151 | gyddum geomore, þætte Grendel wan | in mournful songs, that Grendel fought |
| 152 | hwile wið Hroþgar, heteniðas wæg, | against Hrothgar for a long while, waged hateful malice, |
| 153 | fyrene ond fæhðe fela missera, | crime and feud, for many seasons, |
| 154 | singale sæce; sibbe ne wolde | perpetual strife; he wanted no peace |
| 155 | wið manna hwone mægenes Deniga | with any man of the Danish host, |
| 156 | feorhbealo feorran, feo þingian, | would not remove the deadly evil for payment, |
| 157 | ne þær ænig witena wenan þorfte | nor did any counselor there need to expect |
| 158 | beorhtre bote to banan folmum. | bright compensation at the hands of the killer. |
| 159 | [Atol] æglæca ehtende wæs, | The dread monster pursued them relentlessly, |
| 160 | deorc deaþscua, duguþe ond geogoþe, | dark death-shadow, both veteran and young warrior, |
| 161 | seomade ond syrede, sinnihte heold | lay in ambush and plotted; it held in perpetual night |
| 162 | mistige moras; men ne cunnon, | the misty moors; men do not know |
| 163 | hwyder helrunan hwyrftum scriþað. | where such hell-spirits wander in their course. |
| 164 | Swa fela fyrena feond mancynnes, | So the enemy of mankind |
| 165 | atol angengea, oft gefremede, | committed many crimes, terrible solitary walker, |
| 166 | heardra hynða; Heorot eardode, | cruel humiliations; it occupied Heorot, |
| 167 | sincfage sel sweartum nihtum; | the treasure-adorned hall, in the dark nights; |
| 168 | no he þone gifstol gretan moste, | it might never approach the gift-throne, |
| 169 | maþðum for Metode, ne his myne wisse. | the treasure, because of the Measurer, nor did it know his love. |
| 170 | Þæt wæs wræc micel wine Scyldinga, | That was great misery to the friend of the Scyldings, |
| 171 | modes brecða. Monig oft gesæt | a breaking of the spirit. Many a powerful man often sat |
| 172 | rice to rune, ræd eahtedon, | in council, discussed counsel, |
| 173 | hwæt swiðferhðum selest wære | about what would be best for the strong-hearted |
| 174 | wið færgryrum to gefremmanne. | to do against the sudden terrors. |
| 175 | Hwilum hie geheton æt hærgtrafum | At times they vowed sacrifices at heathen temples, |
| 176 | wigweorþunga, wordum bædon, | prayed with words |
| 177 | þæt him gastbona geoce gefremede | that the soul-slayer would grant them help |
| 178 | wið þeodþreaum. Swylc wæs þeaw hyra, | against the people's afflictions. Such was their custom, |
| 179 | hæþenra hyht; helle gemundon | the hope of heathens; they remembered hell |
| 180 | in modsefan, Metod hie ne cuþon, | in their hearts, they did not know the Measurer, |
| 181 | dæda Demend, ne wiston hie Drihten God, | the Judge of deeds, they did not know the Lord God, |
| 182 | ne hie huru heofena Helm herian ne cuþon, | nor indeed did they know how to praise the Protector of the heavens, |
| 183 | wuldres Waldend. Wa bið þæm ðe sceal | the Ruler of glory. Woe be to him who must, |
| 184 | þurh sliðne nið sawle bescufan | through dire hostility, thrust his soul |
| 185 | in fyres fæþm, frofre ne wenan, | into the fire's embrace, expect no comfort, |
| 186 | wihte gewendan; wel bið þæm þe mot | no change at all; well be it for him who may, |
| 187 | æfter deaðdæge Drihten secean, | after his death-day, seek the Lord, |
| 188 | ond to Fæder fæþmum freoðo wilnian. | and ask for protection in the Father's embrace. |
