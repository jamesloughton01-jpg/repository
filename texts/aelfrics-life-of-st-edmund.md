# Ælfric's Life of St Edmund

Prose hagiography (*Passio Sancti Eadmundi*), written by Ælfric of Eynsham
c. 990s AD, part of his *Lives of Saints* collection. Recounts the
martyrdom of Edmund, King of the East Angles, killed by Viking raiders
(Ivar/Hingwar and Ubba) in 869 AD for refusing to renounce Christianity —
the same Viking incursions referenced in The Battle of Maldon and The
Battle of Brunanburh. Ælfric based it on an earlier Latin account by Abbo
of Fleury. Written in Ælfric's distinctive rhythmical, alliterative prose
style. Numbering below follows the narrative's paragraphs, not manuscript
line numbers, since this is prose rather than verse. Old English text:
Wikisource, from W.W. Skeat's 1881 edition (*Ælfric's Lives of Saints*,
Early English Text Society), public domain; spelling lightly normalized
(wynn → w, scribal abbreviations expanded) for readability. Modern English
translation: own work for this repository.

| 1 | Sum swyþe gelæred munuc com suþan ofer sæ fram sce Benedictes stowe on Æþelredes cynincges dæge to Dunstane ærce-bisceope þrim gearum ær he forðferde, and se munuc hatte Abbo. Þa wurdon hi æt spræce oþþæt Dunstan rehte be sce Eadmunde swa swa Eadmundes swurd-bora hit rehte Æþelstane cynincge þa þa Dunstan iung man wæs, and se swurd-bora wæs forealdod man. | A very learned monk came from the south, over the sea, from St. Benedict's monastery, in King Æthelred's days, to Archbishop Dunstan, three years before Dunstan died; and the monk was called Abbo. They fell into conversation, until Dunstan related the story of St. Edmund, just as Edmund's own sword-bearer had told it to King Æthelstan, when Dunstan was a young man and the sword-bearer was a very old man. |
| 2 | Þa gesette se munuc ealle þa gereccednysse on anre bec, and eft ða þa seo boc com to us binnan feawum gearum þa awende we hit on Englisc, swa swa hit her-æfter stent. | The monk then set down the whole account in a book; and afterward, when that book came to us within a few years, we translated it into English, as it stands hereafter. |
| 3 | Se munuc þa Abbo binnan twam gearum, gewende ham to his mynstre and wearð sona to abbode geset on þa ylcan mynstre. | The monk Abbo, within two years, returned home to his monastery, and was soon made abbot of that same monastery. |
| 4 | Eadmund se eadiga Eastengla cynincg wæs snotor and wurðfull, and wurðode symble mid æþelum þeawum þone ælmihtigan god. | Edmund the blessed, king of the East Angles, was wise and honorable, and always honored almighty God with noble virtues. |
| 5 | He wæs eadmod, and geþungen, and swa anræde þurhwunode þæt he nolde abugan to bysmorfullum leahtrum, ne on naþre healfe he ne ahylde his þeawas, ac wæs symble gemyndig þære soþan lare: gif þu eart to heafod-men geset, ne ahefe þu ðe, ac beo betwux mannum swa swa an man of him. | He was humble and virtuous, and remained so steadfast that he would not bend to shameful vices, nor stray in either direction from his principles, but was always mindful of the true teaching: "If you are set as a leader, do not exalt yourself, but be among men as one of them." |
| 6 | He wæs cystig wædlum and wydewum swa swa fæder, and mid welwillendnysse gewissode his folc symle to rihtwisnysse, and þam reþum styrde, and gesæliglice leofode on soþan geleafan. | He was generous to the poor and to widows, like a father, and with kindness guided his people always toward righteousness, and restrained the cruel, and lived happily in true faith. |
| 7 | Hit gelamp ða æt nextan þæt þa deniscan leode ferdon mid scip-here hergiende and sleande wide geond land swa swa heora gewuna is. | It happened at last that the Danish people went with a fleet, harrying and slaying widely throughout the land, as is their custom. |
| 8 | On þa flotan wæron þa fyrmestan heafod-men Hinguar and Hubba, geanlæhte þurh deofol, and hi on Norðhymbra-lande gelendon mid æscum, and aweston þæt land, and þa leoda ofslogon. | Among that fleet the foremost leaders were Hinguar and Hubba, allied through the devil, and they landed in Northumbria with their warships, laid waste the land, and slew the people. |
| 9 | Þa gewende Hinguar east mid his scipum, and Hubba belaf on Norðhymbra-lande, gewunnenum sige, mid wælhreownysse. | Then Hinguar turned east with his ships, and Hubba remained in Northumbria, having won victory, with cruelty. |
| 10 | Hinguar þa becom to Eastenglum rowende, on þam geare þe Ælfred æðelincg an and twentig geare wæs, se þe Westsexena cynincg siþþan wearð mære; and se fore-sæda Hinguar færlice swa swa wulf on lande bestalcode, and þa leode sloh weras and wif, and þa ungewittigan cild, and to bysmore tucode þa bilewitan cristenan. | Hinguar then came rowing to East Anglia, in the year when the prince Alfred was twenty-one years old — he who afterward became the famous king of the West Saxons. And the aforesaid Hinguar suddenly, like a wolf, stole across the land, and slew the people, men and women and innocent children, and cruelly abused the harmless Christians. |
| 11 | He sende ða sona syððan to þam cyninge beotlic ærende, þæt he abugan sceolde to his man-rædene gif he rohte his feores. | He then soon sent to the king a threatening message, that he must submit to his allegiance, if he valued his life. |
| 12 | Se ærendraca com þa to Eadmunde cynincge and Hinguares ærende him ardlice abead. | The messenger came to King Edmund and quickly delivered Hinguar's message. |
| 13 | "Hinguar ure cyning cene and sigefæst, on sæ and on lande, hæfð fela þeoda gewyld, and com nu mid fyrde færlice her to lande, þæt he her winter-setl mid his werode hæbbe." | "Hinguar our king, bold and victorious on sea and on land, has subdued many nations, and has now suddenly come here with an army, that he might have his winter quarters here with his troop." |
| 14 | "Nu het he þe dælan þine digelan gold-hordas, and þinra yldrena gestreon ardlice wið hine, and þu beo his under-kyning, gif ðu cucu beon wylt, for-ðan-þe ðu næfst þa mihte þæt þu mage him wið-standan." | "Now he commands you to promptly share your hidden gold-hoards and your ancestors' treasures with him, and that you become his under-king, if you wish to remain alive — for you do not have the power to withstand him." |
| 15 | Hwæt þa Eadmund clypode ænne bisceop, þe him þa gehendost wæs, and wið hine smeade hu he þa reþan Hinguare and-wyrdan sceolde. | Then Edmund called for a bishop, who was nearest to him, and discussed with him how he should answer that fierce Hinguar. |
| 16 | Þa forhtode se bisceop for þa færlican gelimpe, and for þæs cynincges life, and cwæð þæt him ræd þuhte þæt he to þam gebuge þe hi bead Hinguar. | The bishop was afraid because of that sudden turn of events, and for the king's life, and said it seemed wise counsel to him that he submit to Hinguar's demand. |
| 17 | Þa suwode se cynincg and beseah to þære eorþan, and cwæð þa æt nextan cynelice him to: | Then the king was silent, and looked to the ground, and then at last spoke to him royally: |
| 18 | "Eala þu bisceop, to bysmore synd getawode þas earman land-leoda, and me nu leofre wære þæt ic on feohte feolle, wið þa þe min folc moste heora eardes brucan." And se bisceop cwæð: | "Ah, bishop, these wretched people of the land have been shamefully treated, and I would now rather fall in battle, so that my people might still enjoy their homeland." And the bishop said: |
| 19 | "Eala þu leofa cyning, þin folc lið ofslagen, and þu næfst þone fultum þæt þu feohtan mæge, and þas flot-men cumað, and þe cucenne gebindað butan þu mid fleame þinum feore gebeorge, oððe þu þe swa gebeorge þæt þu buge to him." | "Ah, beloved king, your people lie slain, and you do not have the strength to fight; these seamen will come and bind you alive, unless you save your life by flight, or save yourself by submitting to him." |
| 20 | Þa cwæð Eadmund cyning swa swa he ful cene wæs: | Then King Edmund spoke, as bold as he was: |
| 21 | "Þæs ic gewilnige and gewisce mid mode, þæt ic ana ne belife æfter minum leofum þegnum þe on heora bedde wurdon mid bearnum, and wifum, færlice ofslægene fram þysum flot-mannum." | "This I desire and wish in my heart: that I should not remain alone, outliving my beloved thegns, who together with their children and wives were suddenly slain in their beds by these pirates." |
| 22 | "Næs me næfre gewunelic þæt ic worhte fleames, ac ic wolde swiðor sweltan gif ic þorfte for minum agenum earde; and se ælmihtiga god wat þæt ic nelle abugan fram his biggengum æfre, ne fram his soþan lufe, swelte ic, lybbe ic." | "It was never my custom to take flight; rather I would sooner die, if I must, for my own homeland. And almighty God knows that I will never turn away from his worship, nor from true love of him, whether I die or live." |
| 23 | Æfter þysum wordum he gewende to þam ærendracan þe Hinguar him to sende, and sæde him unforht: | After these words he turned to the messenger whom Hinguar had sent, and said to him, unafraid: |
| 24 | "Witodlice þu wære wyrðe sleges nu, ac ic nelle afylan on þinum fulum blode mine clænan handa, forðan-þe ic Criste folgie þe us swa gebysnode, and ic bliðelice wille beon ofslagen þurh eow gif hit swa god fore-sceawað." | "Truly you would deserve death now, but I will not stain my clean hands with your foul blood, for I follow Christ, who set us such an example, and I will gladly be slain by you, if God so ordains it." |
| 25 | "Far nu swiþe hraðe, and sege þinum reþan hlaforde: ne abihð næfre Eadmund Hingware on life hæþenum here-togan, buton he to hælende Criste ærest mid ge-leafan on þysum lande gebuge." | "Go now very swiftly, and tell your fierce lord: Edmund will never in his life submit to Hingwar, a heathen war-leader, unless he first submits, with faith, to the Savior Christ in this land." |
| 26 | Þa gewende se ærend-raca ardlice aweg, and gemette be wæge þone wælhreowan Hingwar mid eallre his fyrde fuse to Eadmunde, and sæde þam arleasan hu him gewyrd wæs. | Then the messenger swiftly turned away, and met on the road the cruel Hingwar, hastening toward Edmund with all his army, and told the wicked man how things had gone. |
| 27 | Hingwar þa bebead mid bylde þa scip-here þæt hi þæs cynincges anes ealle cepan sceoldon, þe his hæse forseah, and hine sona bindan. | Hingwar then boldly commanded his fleet that they should all seize that king alone, who had scorned his command, and bind him at once. |
| 28 | Hwæt þa Eadmund cynincg mid þam þe Hingwar com, stod innan his healle þæs hælendes gemyndig, and awearp his wæpna, wolde geæfen-læcan Cristes gebysnungum, þe for-bead Petre mid wæpnum to winnenne wið þa wælhreowan Iudeiscan. | As soon as Hingwar arrived, King Edmund stood within his hall, mindful of the Savior, and cast away his weapons, wishing to follow Christ's example, who forbade Peter to fight with weapons against the cruel Jews. |
| 29 | Hwæt þa arleasan þa Eadmund gebundon, and gebysmrodon huxlice, and beoton mid saglum, and swa syððan læddon þone geleaf-fullan cyning to anum eorð-fæstum treowe, and tigdon hine þær-to mid heardum bendum, and hine eft swuncgon langlice mid swipum, and he symble clypode betwux þa swinglum mid soðan geleafan to hælende Criste, and þa hæþenan þa for his geleafan wurdon wodlice yrre for-þan-þe he clypode Crist him to fultume. | Then the wicked men seized Edmund, mocked him shamefully, and beat him with clubs, and afterward led the faithful king to a tree fixed in the ground, and bound him to it with hard bonds, and scourged him for a long time with whips; and throughout the blows he continually cried out, with true faith, to the Savior Christ. The heathens, because of his faith, grew madly enraged, since he called on Christ for help. |
| 30 | Hi scuton þa mid gafelucum swilce him to gamenes to, oð þæt he eall wæs besæt mid heora scotungum swilce igles byrsta, swa swa Sebastian wæs. | Then they shot at him with javelins, as if for sport, until he was entirely covered with their missiles, like the bristles of a hedgehog — just as [St.] Sebastian was. |
| 31 | Þa geseah Hingwar se arlease flot-man, þæt se æþela cyning nolde Criste wið-sacan, ac mid anrædum geleafan hine æfre clypode; het hine þa beheafdian, and þa hæþenan swa dydon. | Then Hingwar, the wicked pirate, saw that the noble king would not renounce Christ, but with steadfast faith continually called upon him; he then ordered that he be beheaded, and the heathens did so. |
| 32 | Betwux þam þe he clypode to Criste þagit, þa tugon þa hæþenan þone halgan to slæge, and mid anum swencge slogon him of þæt heafod, and his sawl siþode gesælig to Criste. | While he was still calling upon Christ, the heathens dragged the holy man to his death, and with a single stroke struck off his head, and his soul journeyed, blessed, to Christ. |
| 33 | Þær wæs sum man gehende gehealden þurh god, behyd þam hæþenum, þe þis gehyrde eall, and hit eft sæde swa swa we hit secgað her. | There was a certain man nearby, kept safe by God, hidden from the heathens, who heard all this, and afterward told it, just as we tell it here. |
| 34 | Hwæt ða se flot-here ferde eft to scipe, and behyddon þæt heafod þæs halgan Eadmundes on þa þiccum bremelum þæt hit bebyrged ne wurde. | Then the pirate army went back to their ships, and hid the head of the holy Edmund among the thick brambles, so it could not be buried. |
| 35 | Þa æfter fyrste syððan hi afarene wæron, com þæt land-folc to þe þær to lafe wæs þa, þær heora hlafordes lic læg butan heafde, and wurdon swiðe sarige for his slege on mode, and huru þæt hi næfdon þæt heafod to þam bodige. | After a time, once they had departed, the people of the land came to where their lord's body lay, headless, and were greatly grieved in their hearts at his slaying — and especially that they did not have the head to go with the body. |
| 36 | Þa sæde se sceawere þe hit ær geseah, þæt þa flot-men hæfdon þæt heafod mid him, and wæs him geðuht swa swa hit wæs ful soð, þæt hi behyddon þæt heafod on þam holte forhwega. | Then the observer, who had seen it happen, said that the pirates had had the head with them, and it seemed to him — as was quite true — that they had hidden the head somewhere in the wood. |
| 37 | Hi eodon þa secende ealle endemes to þam wuda, secende gehwær geond þyfelas and bremelas gif hi a-hwær mihton gemeton þæt heafod. | They then all went together, searching, into the wood, seeking everywhere among the thickets and brambles, to see if they might anywhere find the head. |
| 38 | Wæs eac micel wundor þæt an wulf wearð asend þurh godes wissunge to bewerigenne þæt heafod wið þa oþre deor, ofer dæg, and niht. | It was also a great wonder that a wolf was sent, through God's guidance, to guard the head against other animals, by day and by night. |
| 39 | Hi eodon þa secende, and symle clypigende, swa swa hit gewunelic is þa ðe on wuda gað oft, "hwær eart þu nu, gefera" — and him andwyrde þæt heafod: | They went about searching, continually calling out, as is customary for those who often go into a wood: "Where are you now, comrade?" — and the head answered them: |
| 40 | "Her, her, her" — and swa gelome clypode andswarigende him eallum, swa oft swa heora ænig clypode, oþþæt hi ealle becomen þurh ða clypunga him to. | "Here, here, here!" — and so it called out repeatedly, answering all of them, as often as any of them called, until they all found their way to it through those callings. |
| 41 | Þa læg se græga wulf þe bewiste þæt heafod, and mid his twam fotum hæfde þæt heafod beclypped, grædig, and hungrig, and for gode ne dorste þæs heafdes abyrian, ac heold hit wið deor. | There lay the grey wolf who guarded the head, holding it clasped between his two paws, greedy and hungry, yet for reverence of God he dared not taste the head, but kept it safe from other animals. |
| 42 | Þa wurdon hi ofwundrode þæs wulfes hyrd-rædenne, and þæt halige heafod ham feredon mid him, þancigende þam ælmihtigan ealra his wundra; ac se wulf folgode forð mid þam heafde, oþþæt hi to tune comon, swylce he tam wære, and gewende eft siþþan to wuda ongean. | They marveled greatly at the wolf's guardianship, and carried the holy head home with them, thanking the Almighty for all his wonders; but the wolf followed along with the head, as if it were tame, until they came to the settlement, and then afterward turned back again into the wood. |
| 43 | Þa land-leoda þa siþþan ledon þæt heafod to þam halgan bodige, and bebyrigdon hine swa swa hi selost mihton on swylcere hrædinge, and cyrcan arærdan sona him onuppon. | The people of the land then laid the head with the holy body, and buried him as best they could in such haste, and soon raised a church over him. |
| 44 | Eft þa on fyrste æfter fela gearum, þa seo hergung geswac and sibb wearð forgifen þam geswenctan folce, þa fengon hi togædere and worhton ane cyrcan wurðlice þam halgan, for-þan-ðe gelome wundra wurdon æt his byrgene æt þam gebæd-huse þær he bebyrgen wæs. | Afterward, after many years, when the raiding ceased and peace was granted to the afflicted people, they joined together and built a church, worthily, for the saint, because frequent miracles occurred at his tomb, at the chapel where he was buried. |
| 45 | Hi woldon þa ferian mid folclicum wurðmynte þone halgan lichaman, and lecgan innan þære cyrcan; þa wæs micel wundor þæt he wæs eall swa gehal swylce he cucu wære mid clænum lichaman, and his swura wæs gehalod þe ær wæs forslagen, and wæs swylce an seolcen þræd embe his swuran ræd mannum to sweotelunge hu he ofslagen wæs. | They then wished to carry the holy body, with public honor, and lay it within the church. Then it was a great wonder that he was entirely whole, as if alive, his body unblemished, and his neck — which had been severed before — was healed, and there was, as it were, a thread of silk around his neck, visible to people as a sign of how he had been killed. |
| 46 | Eac swilce þa wunda þe þa wælhreowan hæþenan mid gelomum scotungum on his lice macodon, wæron gehælede þurh þone heofonlican god, and he liþ swa ansund oþ þisne and-werdan dæg, and-bidigende æristes, and þæs ecan wuldres. | Likewise the wounds which the cruel heathens had made on his body with their repeated arrows were healed through the heavenly God, and he lies just as whole to this present day, awaiting the resurrection and eternal glory. |
| 47 | His lichama us cyð þe lið un-formolsnod þæt he butan forligre her on worulde leofode, and mid clænum life to Criste siþode. | His body, which lies uncorrupted, shows us that he lived here in the world without unchastity, and journeyed to Christ with a pure life. |
| 48 | Sum wudewe wunode Oswyn gehaten æt þæs halgan byrgene on gebedum and fæstenum manega gear syððan; seo wolde efsian ælce geare þone sanct, and his næglas ceorfan syferlice, mid lufe, and on scryne healdan to halig-dome on weofode. | A certain widow, called Oswyn, dwelt near the saint's tomb, in prayers and fastings, for many years afterward; she would trim the saint's hair each year, and cut his nails carefully, with love, and keep them in a shrine as relics upon the altar. |
| 49 | Þa wurðode þæt land-folc mid geleafan þone sanct, and Þeodred bisceop þearle mid gifum on golde and on seolfre, þam sce to wurðmynte. | Then the people of the land honored the saint with faith, and Bishop Theodred greatly, with gifts of gold and silver, to the saint's honor. |
| 50 | Þa comon on sumne sæl unge-sælige þeofas eahta on anre nihte to þam arwurðan halgan, woldon stelan þa maðmas þe men þyder brohton, and cunnodon mid cræfte hu hi in cumon mihton. | Then, at one time, eight unfortunate thieves came in one night to the venerable saint, wishing to steal the treasures that people had brought there, and tried with cunning to find a way in. |
| 51 | Sum sloh mid slecge swiðe þa hæpsan, sum heora mid feolan feolode abutan, sum eac underdealf þa duru mid spade, sum heora mid hlæddre wolde unlucan þæt ægðyrl. | One struck violently at the hasp with a sledgehammer; one of them filed around it with a file; another dug under the door with a spade; another, with a ladder, tried to unlock the window. |
| 52 | Ac hi swuncon on idel, and earmlice ferdon, swa þæt se halga wer hi wundorlice geband, ælcne swa he stod strutigende mid tole, þæt heora nan ne mihte þæt morð gefremman, ne hi þanon astyrian, ac stodon swa oð mergen. | But they labored in vain, and fared miserably, for the holy man wondrously bound them, each one exactly as he stood straining with his tool, so that none of them could carry out that crime, nor move from the spot, but stood so until morning. |
| 53 | Men þa þæs wundrodon hu þa weargas hangodon, sum on hlæddre, sum leat to gedelfe, and ælc on his weorce wæs fæste gebunden. | People marveled at how the criminals hung there — one on the ladder, one bent to his digging — each one fastened firmly at his work. |
| 54 | Hi wurdon þa ge-brohte to þam bisceope ealle, and he het hi hon on heagum gealgum ealle. | They were all then brought before the bishop, and he ordered that they all be hanged on high gallows. |
| 55 | Ac he næs na gemyndig hu se mild-heorta god clypode þurh his witegan þas word þe her standað: | But he was not at all mindful of how the merciful God had spoken, through his prophet, these words which stand here: |
| 56 | *"Eos qui ducuntur ad mortem eruere ne cesses."* (Latin, Proverbs 24:11) | *"Do not cease to deliver those who are being led away to death."* |
| 57 | Þa þe man læt to deaðe alys hi ut symble, and eac þa halgan canones gehadodum forbeodað ge bisceopum ge preostum, to beonne embe þeofas, for-þan-þe hit ne gebyraþ þam þe beoð gecorene gode to þegnigenne þæt hi geþwærlæcan sceolon on æniges mannes deaðe, gif hi beoð drihtnes þenas. | Those who are led to death — always deliver them. And also the holy canons forbid ordained persons, both bishops and priests, to be involved with [the punishment of] thieves, because it is not fitting for those chosen to serve God to consent to any man's death, if they are servants of the Lord. |
| 58 | Eft þa Ðeodred bisceop sceawode his bec syððan, behreowsode mid geomerunge, þæt he swa reðne dom sette þam ungesæligum þeofum, and hit besargode æfre oð his lifes ende, and þa leode bæd georne þæt hi him mid fæstan fullice þry dagas, biddende þone ælmihtigan þæt he him arian scolde. | Afterward Bishop Theodred examined his books, and repented with grief that he had passed such a harsh judgment on those unfortunate thieves, and he regretted it ever after, to the end of his life, and earnestly asked the people to fast with him for three full days, praying to the Almighty for his mercy. |
| 59 | On þam lande wæs sum man, Leofstan gehaten, rice for worulde, and unwittig for gode; se rad to þam halgan mid riccetere swiðe, and het him æt-eowian orhlice swiðe, þone halgan sanct hwæþer he gesund wære; ac swa hraðe swa he geseah þæs sanctes lichaman, þa awedde he sona, and wæl-hreowlice grymetede, and earmlice geendode yfelum deaðe. | In that land there was a man called Leofstan, powerful in the world but foolish before God; he rode to the saint very arrogantly, and insolently commanded that they show him the holy saint, to see whether he was intact. But as soon as he saw the saint's body, he went mad at once, and roared savagely, and wretchedly ended in an evil death. |
| 60 | Þis is ðam gelic þe se geleaffulla papa Gregorius sæde on his gesetnysse be ða halgan Laurentie ðe lið on Rome-byrig, þæt menn woldon sceawian symle hu he lage, ge gode ge yfele, ac god hi ge-stilde, swa þæt þær swulton on þære sceawunge ane seofon menn ætgædere, þa geswicon þa oþre to sceawigenne þone martyr mid menniscum gedwylde. | This is like what the faithful Pope Gregory said in his writing concerning the holy Laurence, who lies in the city of Rome — that people always wanted to see how he lay, both good and evil alike, but God restrained them, so that seven men died together at once during that viewing; then the others ceased to view the martyr out of human presumption. |
| 61 | Fela wundra we gehyrdon on folclicre spræce be þam halgan Eadmunde þe we her nellaþ on gewrite settan, ac hi wat gehwa. | We have heard many wonders, in popular report, concerning the holy Edmund, which we will not set down here in writing — but everyone knows them. |
| 62 | On þyssum halgan is swutel, and on swilcum oþrum, þæt god ælmihtig mæg þone man aræran eft on domes dæg andsundne of eorþan se þe hylt Eadmunde halne his lichaman, oð þone micclan dæg þeah ðe he of moldan come. | In this saint, and in others like him, it is clear that almighty God can raise a person up again, whole, on Judgment Day, out of the earth — he who keeps Edmund's body whole until that great day, even though it came from the earth. |
| 63 | Wyrðe is seo stow for þam wurðfullan halgan þæt hi man wurþige and wel gelogige mid clænum godes þeowum, to Cristes þeow-dome, for-þan-þe se halga is mærra þonne men magon asmeagan. | That place is worthy, for the sake of the honored saint, to be honored and well provided for with pure servants of God, in the service of Christ, because the saint is greater than men can comprehend. |
| 64 | Nis Angel-cynn bedæled drihtnes halgena, þonne on Engla-landa licgaþ swilce halgan swylce þæs halga cyning is and Cuþberht se eadiga, and sce Æþeldryð on Elig, and eac hire swustor ansunde on lichaman geleafan to trymminge. | The English people are not deprived of the Lord's saints, when such saints lie in England as this holy king, and the blessed Cuthbert, and St. Æthelthryth at Ely, and also her sister, uncorrupted in body, for the strengthening of faith. |
| 65 | Synd eac fela oðre on Angel-cynne halgan þe fela wundra wyrcað, swa swa hit wide is cuð þam ælmihtigan to lofe, þe hi on gelyfdon. | There are also many other saints among the English people who work many miracles, as is widely known, to the praise of the Almighty, in whom they believed. |
| 66 | Crist geswutelaþ mannum þurh his mæran halgan þæt he is ælmihtig god þe macað swilce wundra, þeah þe þa earman Iudei hine eallunge wið-socen, for-þan-þe hi synd awyrgede swa swa hi wiscton hi sylfum. | Christ reveals to men, through his glorious saints, that he is the almighty God who works such wonders — even though the wretched Jews entirely denied him, for they are cursed, just as they wished upon themselves. |
| 67 | Ne beoð nane wundra geworhte æt heora byrgenum, for-ðan-þe hi ne gelyfað on þone lifigendan Crist, ac Crist geswutelað mannum hwær se soða geleafa is, þonne he swylce wundra wyrcð þurh his halgan wide geond þas eorðan. | No miracles are worked at their graves, because they do not believe in the living Christ; but Christ reveals to men where true faith lies, when he works such wonders through his saints widely throughout this earth. |
| 68 | Þæs him sy wuldor a mid his heofonlican fæder, and þam halgan gaste, a buton ende. Amen. | For this, may glory be to him always, with his heavenly Father, and the Holy Spirit, forever without end. Amen. |
