# The Wanderer

Preserved in the Exeter Book. Old English text: *Bright's Anglo-Saxon Reader*
(James W. Bright), via Wikisource, CC BY-SA (underlying poem is public domain,
composed by an unknown author, likely 9th-10th century). Modern English
translation: own work for this repository, prose rendering (not verse).

| # | Old English | Modern English |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Oft him ānhaga āre gebīdeð, | Often the solitary one experiences favor for himself, |
| 2 | Metudes miltse, þēah þe he mōdcearig | the mercy of the Measurer, although he, troubled in heart, |
| 3 | geond lagulāde longe sceolde | over the sea-path, for a long time must |
| 4 | hrēran mid hǫndum hrīmcealde sǣ, | stir with his hands the rime-cold sea, |
| 5 | wadan wræclāstas: wyrd bið ful ārǣd! | tread the paths of exile: fate is wholly inexorable! |
| 6 | Swā cwæð eardstapa, earfeþa gemyndig, | So spoke the wanderer, mindful of hardships, |
| 7 | wrāþra wælsleahta, winemǣga hryre: | of cruel slaughters, the downfall of beloved kinsmen: |
| 8 | 'Oft ic sceolde āna ūhtna gehwylce | 'Often I alone had to, every dawn, |
| 9 | mīne ceare cwīþan; nis nū cwicra nān, | lament my care; there is now no one alive |
| 10 | þe ic him mōdsefan mīnne durre | to whom I dare reveal my innermost thoughts |
| 11 | sweotule āsęcgan. Ic tō sōþe wāt | clearly. I know truly |
| 12 | þæt biþ in eorle indryhten þēaw, | that it is a noble custom in a man |
| 13 | þæt hē his ferðlocan fæste binde, | that he bind fast his heart's coffer, |
| 14 | healde his hordcofan, hycge swā hē wille; | hold his treasure-chamber, think as he will; |
| 15 | ne mæg wērig mōd wyrde wiðstǫndan | the weary mind cannot withstand fate, |
| 16 | nē sē hrēo hyge helpe gefręmman: | nor can the troubled spirit provide help: |
| 17 | for ðon dōmgeorne drēorigne oft | therefore those eager for glory often |
| 18 | in hyra brēostcofan bindað fæste. | bind fast their sorrow in their breast. |
| 19 | Swā ic mōdsefan mīnne sceolde, | So I had to bind my own spirit, |
| 20 | oft earmcearig, ēðle bidǣled, | often wretched with care, deprived of my homeland, |
| 21 | frēomǣgum feor feterum sǣlan, | far from noble kinsmen, with fetters, |
| 22 | siþþan gēara iū goldwine mīnne | since long years ago I covered my gold-friend |
| 23 | hrūsan heolster biwrāh, and ic hēan þǫnan | in the darkness of the earth, and I, abject, from there |
| 24 | wōd wintercearig ofer waþema gebind, | went, winter-sad, over the frozen waves, |
| 25 | sōhte sęle drēorig sinces bryttan, | sought, sorrowful, a hall and a dispenser of treasure — |
| 26 | hwǣr ic feor oþþe nēah findan meahte | where, far or near, I might find |
| 27 | þone þe in meoduhealle minne wisse | one who in the mead-hall might know of my people, |
| 28 | oþþe mec frēondlēasne frēfran wolde, | or would wish to comfort me, friendless, |
| 29 | węnian mid wynnum. Wāt sā þe cunnað | accustom me to joys. He who has experienced it knows |
| 30 | hū slīþen bið sorg tō gefēran | how cruel sorrow is as a companion |
| 31 | þām þe him lȳt hafað lēofra geholena: | for the one who has few beloved protectors: |
| 32 | warað hine wræclāst, nāles wunden gold, | the path of exile holds him, not twisted gold, |
| 33 | ferðloca frēorig, nālæs foldan blǣd; | a frozen heart, not earthly glory; |
| 34 | gemǫn hē sęlesęcgas and sincþęge, | he remembers hall-companions and the receiving of treasure, |
| 35 | hū hine on geoguðe his goldwine | how in his youth his gold-friend |
| 36 | węnede tō wiste: wyn eal gedrēas! | accustomed him to feasting: all joy has perished! |
| 37 | For þon wāt sē þe sceal his winedryhtnes | Therefore he knows, he who must, without his beloved lord's |
| 38 | lēofes lārcwidum lǫnge forþolian, | counsels do for a long time, |
| 39 | ðonne sorg and slǣp sǫmod ætgædre | when sorrow and sleep together |
| 40 | earmne ānhagan oft gebindað: | often bind the wretched solitary one: |
| 41 | þinceð him on mōde þæt hē his mǫndryhten | it seems to him in his mind that he embraces |
| 42 | clyppe and cysse, and on cnēo lęcge | and kisses his lord, and lays on his knee |
| 43 | hǫnda and hēafod, swā hē hwīlum ǣr | hands and head, as he sometimes before, |
| 44 | in gēardagum giefstōlas brēac. | in days of yore, enjoyed the gift-seat's bounty. |
| 45 | ðonne onwæcneð eft winelēas guma, | Then the friendless man wakes again, |
| 46 | gesihð him biforan fealwe wægas, | sees before him the fallow waves, |
| 47 | baþian brimfuglas, brǣdan feþra, | sea-birds bathe, spread their feathers, |
| 48 | hrēosan hrīm and snāw hagle gemęnged. | frost and snow fall, mixed with hail. |
| 49 | Þonne bēoð þȳ hęfigran heortan bęnne, | Then the heart's wounds are the heavier, |
| 50 | sāre æfter swǣsne, sorg bið genīwad, | sore with longing for the dear one; sorrow is renewed, |
| 51 | þonne māga gemynd mōd geondhweorfeð, | when the memory of kinsmen passes through the mind; |
| 52 | grēteð glīwstafum, georne geondscēawað | he greets them joyfully, eagerly scans |
| 53 | sęcga geseldan: swimmað eft on weg; | the companions of men: they swim away again; |
| 54 | flēotendra ferð no þǣr fela bringeð | the spirit of the floating ones brings not much there |
| 55 | cūðra cwidegiedda; cearo bið genīwad | of familiar spoken words; care is renewed |
| 56 | þām þe sęndan sceal swīþe geneahhe | for him who must send, very often, |
| 57 | ofer waþema gebind wērigne sefan. | his weary spirit over the binding of the waves. |
| 58 | For þon ic geþęncan ne mæg geond þās woruld | Therefore I cannot think, throughout this world, |
| 59 | for hwan mōdsefa min ne gesweorce, | why my spirit should not darken, |
| 60 | þonne ic eorla līf eal geondþęnce, | when I fully consider the life of men, |
| 61 | hū hī fǣrlīce flęt ofgēafon, | how they suddenly gave up the hall-floor, |
| 62 | mōdge maguþegnas. Swā þēs middangeard | the proud young retainers. So this middle-earth |
| 63 | ealra dōgra gehwām drēoseð and fealleþ; | each and every day declines and falls; |
| 64 | for þon ne mæg weorþan wīs wer, ǣr hā āge | therefore a man cannot become wise, before he has |
| 65 | wintra dǣl in woruldrīce. Wita sceal geþyldig, | a share of years in the world. A wise man must be patient, |
| 66 | nē sceal nō tō hātheort nē tō hrædwyrde, | neither too hot-hearted nor too hasty of speech, |
| 67 | nē tō wāc wiga nē tō wanhȳdig, | nor too weak a warrior nor too reckless, |
| 68 | nē tō forht nē tō fægen nē tō feohgīfre, | nor too fearful nor too joyful nor too greedy for wealth, |
| 69 | nē nǣfre gielpes tō georn, ǣr hē geare cunne. | nor ever too eager to boast, before he truly knows himself. |
| 70 | Beorn sceal gebīdan, þonne hē bēot spriceð, | A man must wait, when he speaks a vow, |
| 71 | oþ þæt collenferð cunne gearwe | until the proud-hearted one knows clearly |
| 72 | hwider hreþra gehygd hweorfan wille. | where the intention of his heart will turn. |
| 73 | Ongietan sceal glēaw hæle hū gæstlīc bið, | A wise man must perceive how ghostly it will be, |
| 74 | þonne eall þisse worulde wela wēste stǫndeð, | when all the wealth of this world stands waste, |
| 75 | swā nū missenlīce geond þisne middangeard | as now, in various places throughout this middle-earth, |
| 76 | winde biwāune weallas stǫndaþ, | walls stand blown by the wind, |
| 77 | hrīme bihrorene, hryðge þā ederas. | covered with frost, the buildings storm-swept. |
| 78 | Wōriað þā wīnsalo, waldend licgað | The wine-halls crumble, the rulers lie |
| 79 | drēame bidrorene; duguð eal gecrǫng | deprived of joy; the whole company has fallen, |
| 80 | wlǫnc bī wealle: sume wīg fornōm, | proud, by the wall: war took away some, |
| 81 | fęrede in forðwege; sumne fugel oþbær | carried them on their way forth; a bird bore one away |
| 82 | ofer hēanne holm; sumne sē hāra wulf | over the high sea; the grey wolf shared one |
| 83 | dēaðe gedǣlde; sumne drēorighlēor | with death; one, sad-faced, |
| 84 | in eorðscræfe eorl gehȳdde. | a man hid in an earth-grave. |
| 85 | Ȳþde swā þisne eardgeard ælda Scyppend | Thus the Creator of men laid waste this dwelling-place, |
| 86 | oþ þæt burgwara breahtma lēase | until, void of the noise of its inhabitants, |
| 87 | eald ęnta geweorc īdlu stōdon. | the old work of giants stood empty. |
| 88 | Sē þonne þisne wealsteal wīse, geþōhte | He who then wisely considered this foundation of walls, |
| 89 | and þis deorce līf dēope geondþęnceð, | and deeply ponders this dark life, |
| 90 | frōd in ferðe feor oft gemǫn | wise in spirit, often remembers, far back, |
| 91 | wælsleahta worn, and þās word ācwið: | a multitude of slaughters, and speaks these words: |
| 92 | 'Hwǣr cwōm mearg? hwǣr cwōm mago? hwǣr cwōm māþþumgyfa? | 'Where has the horse gone? Where the young man? Where the treasure-giver? |
| 93 | hwǣr cwōm symbla gesetu? hwǣr sindon sęledrēamas? | Where has the seat of feasting gone? Where are the hall-joys? |
| 94 | Ēalā beorht bune! ēalā byrnwiga! | Alas, bright cup! Alas, mailed warrior! |
| 95 | ēalā þēodnes þrym! hū sēo þrāg gewāt, | Alas, the prince's glory! How that time has passed, |
| 96 | genāp under nihthelm, swā hēo nō wǣre! | grown dark under the helm of night, as if it had never been! |
| 97 | Stǫndeð nū on lāste lēofre duguþe | There stands now, in the place of that beloved company, |
| 98 | weal wundrum hēah, wyrmlīcum fāh: | a wall, wondrously high, decorated with serpent-shapes: |
| 99 | eorlas fornōmon asca þrȳþe, | the might of spears took away the warriors, |
| 100 | wǣpen wælgīfru, wyrd sēo mǣre; | weapons greedy for slaughter, fate the mighty; |
| 101 | and þās stānhleoþu stormas cnyssað; | and storms beat these rocky slopes; |
| 102 | hrīð hrēosende hrūsan bindeð, | falling snow binds the earth, |
| 103 | wintres wōma, þonne wǫn cymeð, | the terror of winter, when darkness comes, |
| 104 | nīpeð nihtscūa, norþan onsęndeð | night's shadow grows dark, sends from the north |
| 105 | hrēo hæglfare hæleþum on andan. | a fierce hailstorm in hostility to men. |
| 106 | Eall is earfoðlīc eorþan rīce, | All is full of hardship in the kingdom of earth, |
| 107 | onwęndeð wyrda gesceaft weoruld under heofonum: | the decree of fate changes the world under the heavens: |
| 108 | hēr bið feoh lǣne, hēr bið frēond lǣne, | here wealth is fleeting, here friend is fleeting, |
| 109 | hēr bið mǫn lǣne, hēr bið mǣg lǣne; | here man is fleeting, here kinsman is fleeting; |
| 110 | eal þis eorþan gesteal īdel weorþeð!' | all this foundation of earth will become empty!' |
| 111 | Swā cwæð snottor on mōde, gesæt him sundor æt rūne. | So spoke the wise man in his mind, sat apart in private thought. |
| 112 | Til biþ sē þe his trēowe gehealdeð: ne sceal nǣfre his torn tō rycene | Good is he who keeps his faith: he must never his grief too quickly |
| 113 | beorn of his brēostum ācȳþan, nemþe hē ǣr þā bōte cunne, | reveal from his breast, unless he already knows the remedy, |
| 114 | eorl mid ęlne gefręmman. Wel bið þām þe him āre sēceð, | a man must achieve it with courage. Well is it for him who seeks favor, |
| 115 | frōfre tō Fæder on heofonum, þǣr ūs eal sēo fæstnung stǫndeð. | comfort from the Father in heaven, where all security stands for us. |
