# Solomon and Saturn

**Solomon and Saturn** is the umbrella name for four separate Old English
works pairing King Solomon against a pagan figure named Saturn in a
dialogue of wisdom and riddles. This collection includes the first and
most self-contained of the four — the poem scholars call **Solomon and
Saturn I** — in full (180 lines). It survives in two fragmentary
manuscripts, both at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge: MS 422 (the
fuller copy, its first page damaged to near-illegibility) and, in the
margin of MS 41's copy of Alfred's translation of Bede, a 189-line
fragment covering the opening. The two other poems grouped under the
name — Solomon and Saturn II, a longer and more disjointed riddle
dialogue that follows directly after this one in the manuscript, and a
separate prose Solomon and Saturn — are not included here, to keep this
to one complete, coherent unit rather than an assortment of fragments.

Solomon and Saturn I personifies the Latin Pater Noster prayer as a
warrior whose letters, individually, do battle with the devil — each
runic character named in the poem (ᛈ P, ᚪ A, ᛏ T, etc.) physically
attacks and tortures him in turn. It's one of the more unusual survivals
of Old English religious poetry, blending Christian devotion with the
combat imagery of heroic verse. Two short passages are damaged beyond
recovery in the manuscript (marked with an ellipsis below); everything
else is a complete reading. Old English text: John M. Kemble, *The
Dialogue of Salomon and Saturnus, with an Historical Introduction*
(London: Ælfric Society, 1848), public domain (published well over 150
years ago); spelling given in the standard modern editorial convention
(þ/ð, macrons omitted to match this repository's house style) since
Kemble's own blackletter typeface does not OCR reliably — cross-checked
line by line against a second, independently-transcribed digital copy
of the same manuscript reading for accuracy. Modern English translation:
own work for this repository, prose rendering (not verse). Numbering
follows this edition's own line count, not a later critical edition's.
