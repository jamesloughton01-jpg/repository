# The Wife's Lament

Preserved in the Exeter Book, folio 115a, 53 lines, probably composed in
the 9th or 10th century. An elegy spoken by a woman exiled and separated
from her husband through the scheming of his kinsmen, now living out her
sorrow alone in a cave-like dwelling under an oak tree. One of only a
handful of surviving Old English poems narrated by a female speaker. Old
English text: cross-checked against Kluge's *Angelsächsisches Lesebuch*
(3rd ed., 1902, Halle: Max Niemeyer), a public-domain scholarly edition
(over 120 years old; consistent with the standard manuscript reading found
across editions of the Exeter Book). Modern English translation: own work
for this repository, prose rendering (not verse).

|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ic þis giedd wrece bi me ful geomorre, | I compose this song about myself, full of sorrow, |
| 2 | minre sylfre sið. Ic þæt secgan mæg, | about my own journey. I can say this: |
| 3 | hwæt ic yrmþa gebad, siþþan ic up weox, | what hardships I have endured since I grew up, |
| 4 | niwes oþþe ealdes, no ma þonne nu. | new ones or old, never more than now. |
| 5 | A ic wite wonn minra wræcsiþa. | Ever have I suffered torment from my exile-journeys. |
| 6 | ærest min hlaford gewat heonan of leodum | First my lord departed from here, from his people, |
| 7 | ofer yþa gelac; hæfde ic uhtceare | over the tossing waves; I had dawn-sorrow, |
| 8 | hwær min leodfruma londes wære. | wondering where in the land my lord might be. |
| 9 | ða ic me feran gewat folgað secan, | Then I set out myself to seek his following, |
| 10 | wineleas wrecca, for minre weaþearfe. | a friendless exile, because of my dire need. |
| 11 | Ongunnon þæt þæs monnes magas hycgan | That man's kinsmen began to plot, |
| 12 | þurh dyrne geþoht, þæt hy todælden unc, | through secret intent, to separate the two of us, |
| 13 | þæt wit gewidost in woruldrice | so that we, as far apart as possible in this world, |
| 14 | lifdon laðlicost, ond mec longade. | should live most hatefully — and longing seized me. |
| 15 | Het mec hlaford min herheard niman, | My lord commanded me to take up a dwelling in a wood, |
| 16 | ahte ic leofra lyt on þissum londstede, | I had few dear ones in this land, |
| 17 | holdra freonda. Forþon is min hyge geomor, | few loyal friends — therefore my heart is sorrowful, |
| 18 | ða ic me ful gemæcne monnan funde, | since I had found a man fully suited to me, |
| 19 | heardsæligne, hygegeomorne, | ill-fated, sorrowful in mind, |
| 20 | mod miþendne, morþor hycgendne. | hiding his thoughts, plotting violence. |
| 21 | Bliþe gebæro ful oft wit beotedan | With a cheerful bearing, very often the two of us vowed |
| 22 | þæt unc ne gedælde nemne deað ana | that nothing but death alone would part us — |
| 23 | owiht elles; eft is þæt onhworfen, | nothing else. Now that has been overturned; |
| 24 | is nu swa hit no wære | it is now as if it never were, |
| 25 | freondscipe uncer. Sceal ic feor ge neah | our friendship. Far and near I must |
| 26 | mines felaleofan fæhðu dreogan. | endure the enmity of my dearly beloved. |
| 27 | Heht mec mon wunian on wuda bearwe, | I was ordered to live in a forest grove, |
| 28 | under actreo in þam eorðscræfe. | under an oak tree, in that cave in the earth. |
| 29 | Eald is þes eorðsele, eal ic eom oflongad, | Old is this earth-hall; I am consumed with longing; |
| 30 | sindon dena dimme, duna uphea, | the valleys are dark, the hills high, |
| 31 | bitre burgtunas, brerum beweaxne, | bitter, walled places, overgrown with briars, |
| 32 | wic wynna leas. Ful oft mec her wraþe begeat | a joyless dwelling. Very often here the departure |
| 33 | fromsiþ frean. Frynd sind on eorþan, | of my lord has seized me cruelly. There are friends on earth, |
| 34 | leofe lifgende, leger weardiað, | living and dear, who lie together in their beds, |
| 35 | þonne ic on uhtan ana gonge | while I, at dawn, walk alone |
| 36 | under actreo geond þas eorðscrafu. | under the oak tree, through these earth-caves. |
| 37 | þær ic sittan mot sumorlangne dæg, | There I must sit the summer-long day, |
| 38 | þær ic wepan mæg mine wræcsiþas, | there I may weep for my exile-journeys, |
| 39 | earfoþa fela; forþon ic æfre ne mæg | my many hardships; and so I can never |
| 40 | þære modceare minre gerestan, | find rest from that grief of mind, |
| 41 | ne ealles þæs longaþes þe mec on þissum life begeat. | nor from all the longing that has taken hold of me in this life. |
| 42 | A scyle geong mon wesan geomormod, | A young person must always be sorrowful in mind, |
| 43 | heard heortan geþoht, swylce habban sceal | hard in the thought of the heart; and likewise must keep |
| 44 | bliþe gebæro, eac þon breostceare, | a cheerful bearing, even alongside sorrow of heart, |
| 45 | sinsorgna gedreag, sy æt him sylfum gelong | a throng of endless griefs — whether all his worldly joy |
| 46 | eal his worulde wyn, sy ful wide fah | depends on himself alone, or whether he is an exile, far and wide, |
| 47 | feorres folclondes, þæt min freond siteð | banished from a distant homeland — just as my beloved sits |
| 48 | under stanhliþe storme behrimed, | under a rocky cliff, rimed with storm, |
| 49 | wine werigmod, wætre beflowen | a weary-hearted friend, surrounded by water |
| 50 | on dreorsele. Dreogeð se min wine | in a sorrowful hall. My friend endures |
| 51 | micle modceare; he gemon to oft | great grief of mind; he remembers too often |
| 52 | wynlicran wic. Wa bið þam þe sceal | a happier dwelling. Woe is it for the one who must, |
| 53 | of langoþe leofes abidan. | out of longing, wait for a beloved. |
